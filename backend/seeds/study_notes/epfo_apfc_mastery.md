# How to use this book

This book is engineered for **one outcome**: scoring **≥ 90%** in the UPSC EPFO APFC Recruitment Test, on the **non-aptitude** sections. Aptitude (Quantitative + English + Reasoning) is covered in three separate Pariksha365 books — `arithmetic`, `english`, `reasoning`. Treat them as siblings.

What makes this book different from the 342-page reference book you already have:

- **Narrative over bullets.** Every chapter is a *story* of a topic — why it exists, the problem it solved, the mental model you should carry, then the facts. Reading a story etches; reading bullets evaporates.
- **One mental model per chapter.** You leave each chapter with a single image you can sketch on a napkin. The 30 facts you also memorise hang off that image.
- **Less repetition, deliberately.** If a concept appears in Chapter 3, it is referenced from Chapter 7 — not re-explained. Re-explanation feels reassuring but produces *familiarity, not recall*.
- **Trap pairs surfaced explicitly.** Every confusion the exam exploits (PoW threshold vs MW threshold, Sec 7A vs 7Q vs 14B, DPSP vs FR) is shown side-by-side with the cue that resolves it.
- **Active recall built in.** Every chapter ends with five questions. If you can't answer them in 60 seconds, you haven't learnt the chapter — re-read the affected section, not the whole thing.

**Read in order.** Each chapter assumes the previous one. The mental model in Chapter 1 (EPF Ecosystem) is needed in Chapter 2 (Labour Law Universe), which is needed in Chapter 3 (Wages-Welfare), and so on.

**Spaced revision schedule.** Read Day 1, revise Day 3, revise Day 9, revise Day 25. The chapter takes 30 minutes the first time; 8 minutes on revision; 3 minutes the fourth time. That's how it lasts.

# CHAPTER 1 — The EPF Ecosystem

> **What you must know after reading this chapter:** the lifecycle of a single rupee that an employee earns — where it splits, who collects it, who promises what when the employee retires/dies/leaves — and why the EPF Act 1952 was even needed.

## 1.0 The 60-second picture

In 1952, when the EPF & MP Act was passed, India faced a quiet crisis: an industrial worker in a Bombay textile mill or a Bengal jute factory had **no cushion** when his working life ended. He would simply stop earning and lean on his sons. If he died young, his widow had nothing. The government solution was elegant — **make the worker save now**, **make the employer match it**, **lock that pool**, and **promise three things**: a lump sum when you retire, a monthly pension to your family, and a free life-insurance cover while you work. That triad — **Provident Fund + Pension + Insurance** — is the EPF Ecosystem.

Every rule in the next 200 pages of the Act is a refinement of *how* to do those three things fairly.

## 1.1 The mental model — three taps and one tank

Picture a wage of **₹15,000** arriving each month. From it, **two taps** pour into a single tank (the EPFO):

```
            employer 12% contribution                  employee 12% contribution
                          ↘                              ↙
                           ↘                            ↙
                        ┌───────────────────────────────────┐
                        │             EPFO TANK             │
                        └───────────────────────────────────┘
                                       ↓ split ↓
              ┌─────────────────┬──────────────────┬──────────────────┐
              ▼                 ▼                  ▼                  ▼
        EPF (savings)      EPS (pension)      EDLI (insurance)    Admin
       3.67% emp share    8.33% emp share     0.50% emp share    0.50% emp share
       + 12% from         (capped at          (capped at         (capped at
        employee          ₹15,000 = ₹1,250)   ₹15,000 = ₹75)     ₹75)
```

Hold this picture in your mind. Every numerical question on EPF, EPS or EDLI is solved by asking: *"Which tap, which sub-account, what cap?"*

The four sub-accounts each have a personality:
- **EPF** — the worker's piggy bank. Compounds at the EPF interest rate (8.25% for FY 2024-25). Returned as a lump sum.
- **EPS** — the pension promise. Pays a monthly pension after 58, calculated by a formula (we'll meet it shortly).
- **EDLI** — the insurance. Pays the family up to **₹7 lakh** if the worker dies *in service*.
- **Admin** — EPFO's running cost. Worker never sees it.

That single tank diagram is your scaffold for the entire EPF Ecosystem.

## 1.2 The story

**Step 1 — Who is covered?** Any establishment in **Schedule I** of the Act with **20 or more employees** is mandatorily covered. Smaller establishments can join voluntarily under **Section 1(4)**. Once covered, the establishment cannot un-cover itself even if numbers drop below 20 — coverage is *sticky*.

**Step 2 — Who pays?** **Both** the employee and the employer pay **12%** of *basic wages + DA + retaining allowance* each month. Note carefully — house rent allowance, bonus, overtime, and commissions are excluded from the contribution base. This single rule has produced more litigation than any other (see *Surya Roshni*, 2019, where the SC held that all *universally paid* allowances are part of basic wages, sweeping in many "allowances" employers had used to dodge contributions).

<div class="examtip">⚠️ **TRAP — PF contribution is 12% of Basic + DA, NOT Gross Salary:** This is the most common numerical trap in APFC papers. Gross salary includes HRA, bonus, overtime, transport allowance etc. — none of these form the contribution base. The base is **Basic wages + Dearness Allowance + Retaining Allowance** only. If a question gives Gross = ₹30,000 with Basic = ₹18,000 and DA = ₹5,000, the contribution base is ₹23,000, NOT ₹30,000.</div>

<div class="keypoint">**Mnemonic — EPFO's Ministry:** EPFO is under the **Ministry of Labour and Employment** (MoLE). Remember: **"Labour Laws = Labour Ministry"**. Examiners sometimes offer "Ministry of Finance" or "Ministry of Commerce" as trap options. The Central Board of Trustees (CBT) is chaired by the **Union Minister of Labour and Employment** — same ministry.</div>

**Step 3 — How does the employer's 12% split?** Of that 12%:
- **8.33%** goes to **EPS** (the pension scheme) — but only up to a wage of **₹15,000** (i.e. max ₹1,250 per month).
- **The remainder** (12% − 8.33% = 3.67% if wage ≤ ₹15,000; or the full 12% if wage exceeds the cap and the worker has *not* opted for higher pension) goes to **EPF**.
- Separately, the employer pays **0.50% as EDLI** (capped at ₹75) and **0.50% as Admin** (capped at ₹75). The employee pays nothing extra towards EDLI or Admin.

**Step 4 — When can the worker get the money out?**
- **EPF**: full lump sum at retirement (age 58). Partial advances are permitted for housing, marriage, illness, education — the EPFO has progressively liberalised these. As of 2025, advance claims up to ₹5 lakh are auto-settled.
- **EPS**: minimum **10 years** of contribution required for a pension at 58. Less than 10 years = a withdrawal benefit (Table D) instead of a pension.
- **EDLI**: payable to the nominee if the worker dies *while in service*. Maximum ₹7 lakh.

**Step 5 — What is the pension formula?**
$$\text{Monthly Pension} = \frac{\text{Pensionable Salary} \times \text{Pensionable Service}}{70}$$

If pensionable salary = ₹15,000 and service = 35 years, monthly pension = 15,000 × 35 / 70 = **₹7,500**. Plain. The whole edifice of the Sunil Kumar 2022 controversy is about whether "pensionable salary" can be the worker's *actual* salary (say ₹60,000) or remains capped at ₹15,000 — the Supreme Court allowed the worker to *opt in* for higher pension on actual salary, subject to paying the differential contribution retrospectively.

**Step 6 — Who enforces this?** The **APFC / RPFC** — that is your future self. Under **Section 7A**, the APFC sits as a quasi-judicial officer and *determines the amount due* from a defaulting employer. **Section 7Q** stacks **12% p.a. interest** on the defaulted amount (mandatory, not discretionary). **Section 14B** stacks **damages** (5-25% under the old slab; uniform 1% per month after 15 June 2024 SC ruling). **Section 14** is the criminal prosecution route — fine and/or imprisonment.

## 1.4 How these are tested

Four question patterns recur:

**Pattern A — "Which is NOT a scheme/sub-account under EPF Act 1952?"** Tests whether you know the *three* schemes (EPF, EPS, EDLI). Wrong options will list defunct schemes (Family Pension Scheme 1971, now subsumed into EPS), Ayushman Bharat (not under EPFO), or APY (under PFRDA, not EPFO).

**Pattern B — "Mr X earns ₹20,000/month. Compute his EPS contribution."** Tests the **cap rule**. Employer's EPS = 8.33% of ₹15,000 (not ₹20,000) = ₹1,250. Always check the cap first.

**Pattern C — "After how many years of service is one eligible for EPS pension?"** Pure recall: **10 years**.

**Pattern D — Statement-based on Sections 7A/7Q/14B.** "Which statement is correct? (i) 7Q interest is discretionary; (ii) 14B damages are mandatory; (iii) burden of proof is on employer." Resolve each by the section's *one-line job* in the table above.

## 1.5 Trap pairs

| The pair | The cue that resolves it |
|---|---|
| EPF (savings) vs EPS (pension) | EPF returns *all your money*; EPS returns *a monthly cheque* |
| Section 7Q (interest) vs Section 14B (damages) | 7Q is **always 12% p.a.**, no slab. 14B is the *penal* charge, now uniformly 1% per month |
| Section 14 (prosecution) vs Section 7A (determination) | 14 = criminal court (jail). 7A = APFC's chair (money) |
| EPFO vs PFRDA | EPFO runs **EPF/EPS/EDLI**. PFRDA runs **NPS/APY**. Different organisations |
| Wage ceiling for coverage vs ceiling for EPS calc | Same number (₹15,000), but the first triggers *whether the worker is covered*, the second caps *how much pension he can earn* |
| Sunil Kumar 2022 vs original EPS | Original capped pensionable salary at ₹15K; Sunil Kumar opens the door to use *actual salary* if the worker pays differential past contributions |

## 1.6 Self-test (60 seconds each)

1. Employer's outflow as a *percentage of an employee's wage* (assume wage ₹15,000) — what is it, broken down by sub-account?
2. An employee dies in service after 4 years of work. What is he/his family eligible for from the EPF Ecosystem?
3. APFC issues a Section 7A order for ₹5 lakh default. The employer pays 90 days late. What is the additional liability?
4. Why does the EPS pension formula divide by **70**, not by 12 or 100?
5. Which section is invoked when an employer's default is discovered *years later* (i.e. after the fact)?

**Answers below — try them first.**

1. 12% employer total (split: 8.33% EPS + 3.67% EPF) + 0.50% EDLI + 0.50% Admin = **13.0% of wage**.
2. EPF lump sum (his accumulated balance + interest) + EDLI insurance (death in service, up to ₹7 lakh). **No pension** (less than 10 yrs service); his family may get a *withdrawal benefit* under EPS Table D instead.
3. 7Q interest at 12% p.a. for 90 days on ₹5 lakh ≈ ₹14,750. *Plus* 14B damages at 1% per month for 3 months = ₹15,000. Roughly **₹30,000 extra** on top of the ₹5 lakh.
4. The denominator 70 = 35 years of pensionable service × 2. It produces a maximum pension of 50% of pensionable salary if the worker works the full 35 years. The "2" is the actuarial scaling factor chosen by the original EPS designers in 1995.
5. **Section 7C** — escaped amount. (7A is the primary determination; 7B is review on new facts; 7C catches genuinely *later-discovered* defaults).

# CHAPTER 2 — The Labour Law Universe

> **What you must know after reading this chapter:** the *purpose* of each major labour law, how they relate to each other, and why the 2020 codification was needed. By the end, when an exam Q says "the Industrial Disputes Act 1947" you should hear in your head *"this is the law about how strikes and dismissals are handled — let me check what specifically is being asked"*, not panic.

## 2.0 The 60-second picture

Indian labour law, from 1923 to 2020, is the story of the **State trying to balance three actors** — the worker, the employer, and the public interest in industrial peace. Each Act in our syllabus was passed because one of those three was being short-changed. The Industrial Disputes Act 1947 was passed because *strikes were paralysing the economy* after Independence. The Trade Unions Act 1926 was passed because *worker collectives kept being treated as criminal conspiracies*. The Factories Act 1948 was passed because *Bombay mill workers were losing limbs and dying*. The Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act 1946 was passed because *employers were firing workers without telling them the rules*.

By 2020, India had **29 separate central labour laws**, plus state variants. The four **Labour Codes** (Wages 2019, IR 2020, OSH 2020, SS 2020) collapsed those 29 into 4. This chapter is the **conceptual map** of the IR group — the laws about how the workplace is governed, how disputes are resolved, how unions function, and how workers know their rights.

## 2.1 The mental model — the workplace as a courtroom

Imagine a factory. Inside, there are **three theatres** of conflict potential:

So the exam still tests both — the Act provisions AND the Code's headline changes.

## 2.2 Theatre 1 — Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act, 1946

**The problem it solved.** Before 1946, a factory worker often did not know whether he was a permanent or temporary employee, what hours he had to work, how he could take leave, or under what circumstances he could be dismissed. The rules were in the employer's head, mutable. **Standing Orders** are *written, certified, posted-on-the-wall rules* that bind both sides.

**Who is covered.** Any **industrial establishment with 100 or more workmen** (lowered to 50 in some states; the IR Code 2020 sets the threshold at **300**). Within 6 months of the Act becoming applicable, the employer must submit *draft* standing orders for **certification** to the Certifying Officer (an officer of the labour department).

**Subjects compulsorily covered.** The Schedule lists 11 matters that standing orders MUST cover, e.g.: classification of workmen (permanent, temporary, badli, apprentice), holidays and leave, shift working, attendance and late-coming, conditions for grant of leave, termination of employment, suspension, misconduct, grievance redress.

**When they come into force.** Standing orders come into operation **30 days after the Certifying Officer's order is final**, OR **7 days after the appellate authority's order, whichever applies**. That's the EO/AO 2023 favourite number.

**Why the exam loves this Act.** It rewards precise recall: the **11 Schedule subjects**, the **employee count threshold** (100 → 300 under IR Code), and the **30-day commencement rule**. Any Q on "model standing orders" is from this Act.

## 2.3 Theatre 2 — Trade Unions Act, 1926

**The problem it solved.** In the 1920s, unions of workers were technically *unlawful combinations* under English common law (the Conspiracy Doctrine). Workers organising a strike could be jailed for criminal conspiracy. The Act gave unions a **legal personality** and **immunities**.

**Registration.** **Any seven** or more members of a trade union, by subscribing their names to its rules, can apply to the Registrar for registration. Registration is **voluntary** — an unregistered union is not illegal, just lacks the privileges. Once registered, the union becomes a **body corporate** with perpetual succession.

**The "ten percent or one hundred" rule (post-2001 amendment).** No trade union shall be registered unless at least **10% of the workmen** or **100 workmen**, whichever is less, are members at the time of application — and at least **7** members must be employed in the establishment.

**Chapter III — Rights and Privileges of Registered Unions.** These are the immunities exam favourite:
- **Sec 17** — immunity from criminal conspiracy charges for acts in furtherance of a trade dispute
- **Sec 18** — immunity from civil suits for inducing breach of contract, etc.
- Power to constitute a **general fund** for objects of the union; a **political fund** is permitted but only members who *opt in* contribute
- Power to hold property in the union's name
- *Not* an immunity: criminal acts (violence, assault, intimidation) — these remain prosecutable

**Trade dispute — definition (Sec 2(g)).** Any dispute between (a) employers and workmen, (b) workmen and workmen, or (c) employers and employers, which is *connected with the employment or non-employment, or terms of employment, or conditions of labour, of any person*.

That word *non-employment* is a frequent trap — it includes disputes about *not* being employed (recruitment denial, refusal to re-employ), not just *being* employed.

**The Central Federations to memorise** (this is the Match the Following favourite):

| Federation | Acronym | Year founded | Political affiliation |
|---|---|---|---|
| All India Trade Union Congress | **AITUC** | **1920** (the OLDEST) | CPI (originally Congress, split 1947) |
| Hind Mazdoor Sabha | HMS | 1948 | Socialist Party (now non-affiliated) |
| Indian National Trade Union Congress | INTUC | 1947 | Indian National Congress |
| United Trade Union Congress | UTUC | 1949 | Revolutionary Socialist Party (RSP) |
| Bharatiya Mazdoor Sangh | BMS | **1955** | RSS/BJP (now LARGEST membership-wise) |
| Centre of Indian Trade Unions | CITU | **1970** | CPI(M) |
| Self-Employed Women's Association | SEWA | 1972 (Ahmedabad) | Independent (founded by Ela Bhatt) |
| Hind Mazdoor Kisan Panchayat | HMKP | 1948 (split) | Praja Socialist Party |
| Trade Union Coordination Committee | TUCC | 1970 | Forward Bloc |

**Chronological order** (the APFC 2023 trap): AITUC (1920) → INTUC (1947) → HMS (1948) → UTUC (1949) → BMS (1955) → CITU (1970) → SEWA (1972). Memorise this sequence — it is the exact ordering Qs ask.

**Trade Union Act vs Industrial Disputes Act — the trap.** Don't confuse the **trade dispute** definition (under TU Act, Sec 2(g)) with **industrial dispute** (under IDA, Sec 2(k)). Same words for similar concepts, two different statutes.

## 2.4 Theatre 3 — Industrial Disputes Act, 1947

**The problem it solved.** Independence-era India inherited the Trade Disputes Act 1929 (a colonial law). After 1947, with industrial conflict surging, Parliament passed the IDA — a *much* more interventionist framework letting the State step into disputes via conciliation, adjudication and awards.

**The two key definitions in Sec 2:**
- **Industrial dispute (2(k)):** dispute between employers and workmen, or workmen and workmen, regarding employment / non-employment / terms / conditions. (Three-pronged like the trade-union definition, but the **Bangalore Water Supply v Rajappa** 1978 7-judge SC ruling gave it a uniquely wide ambit using the **Triple Test**: systematic activity + cooperation between employer and employee + production of goods/services.)
- **Workman (2(s)):** any person employed in any industry to do manual, unskilled, skilled, technical, operational, clerical or supervisory work for hire, *excluding* those mainly in managerial/administrative capacity drawing > ₹10,000/month, and certain other categories.

**Public Utility Services — Sec 2(n) and First Schedule.** Six categories: railways, postal/telegraph/telephone, *industries supplying power/light/water to the public*, *systems of public conservancy or sanitation*, certain *industries notified by appropriate government* (e.g. coal, oil), and *banking* (in some states). Public utility services attract **stricter strike-notice requirements** — see below.

**The 6-week notice rule (the exam darling).** Under Sec 22, in a **public utility service**:
- A worker must give **not less than 6 weeks notice** before going on strike.
- An employer must give **not less than 6 weeks notice** before declaring a lock-out.
- This is in addition to the requirement that strikes/lockouts must not happen during conciliation or for 7 days after.

(Note: the 6-week rule applies to *both* parties — the EO/AO 2025 trap.)

**Dispute settlement hierarchy.** A live dispute moves through a ladder of forums:

```
   1. Works Committee  (Sec 3)  ──→ in-house resolution; only joint committees in establishments with 100+ workmen
              ↓
   2. Conciliation Officer (Sec 4) ──→ mediation; outcome can be a settlement
              ↓
   3. Board of Conciliation (Sec 5) ──→ for complex disputes
              ↓
   4. Voluntary Arbitration (Sec 10A) ──→ both sides agree to a private arbitrator
              ↓
   5. Court of Inquiry (Sec 6) ──→ fact-finding only
              ↓
   6. Labour Court (Sec 7) ──→ adjudicates disputes about dismissal, discipline (Second Schedule matters)
              ↓
   7. Industrial Tribunal (Sec 7A) ──→ adjudicates wages, hours, bonus, gratuity (Third Schedule matters)
              ↓
   8. National Tribunal (Sec 7B) ──→ disputes of *national* importance
```

The **award** of a Labour Court / Tribunal / National Tribunal is binding on the parties.

**Grievance Redressal Committee (Sec 9C — inserted 2010).** Every industrial establishment employing **20 or more workmen** shall have one. Equal representation from employer and workmen, max **10 members**, **adequate representation of women**, term **3 years**. Aggrieved workman must approach within **1 year** of cause of action. (EO/AO 2023 favourite Q.)

**Legal representation before Labour Court / Industrial Tribunal / National Tribunal — Sec 36(4).** A party may be represented by a lawyer **only with the consent of the other parties to the proceedings AND with the leave of the court / tribunal**. So legal representation is **not an automatic right** — both consent of opposite party *and* leave of court are needed. (EO/AO 2023 exact framing.)

**Retrenchment compensation — Sec 25F.** Compensation = **15 days' average pay for every completed year of continuous service**, plus 1 month's notice or pay in lieu. Threshold: applies to workmen who have completed **240 days of continuous service** in the preceding 12 months in an establishment with **100+ workmen** (the IR Code raises this to **300**).

**Lay-off vs Retrenchment vs Closure — the absolute exam favourite trap.**

| Concept | Sec | Trigger | Compensation | Permission needed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Lay-off** | 2(kkk), 25C | *Temporary* inability to give work (power shortage, raw material) — worker remains on rolls | 50% of basic + DA for the lay-off days | Permission needed only if 100+ workmen (300 under Code) |
| **Retrenchment** | 2(oo), 25F | *Termination* of service for any reason other than punishment | 15 days' avg pay × yrs of service + notice | Same threshold |
| **Closure** | 2(cc), 25-O | *Permanent* shutdown of undertaking | 15 days' avg pay × yrs of service (subject to 60 days' notice) | Govt permission if 100+ workmen (300 under Code) |

The cue: *lay-off = temporary*; *retrenchment = individual termination*; *closure = whole shop dies*.

**Conciliation proceedings — when do they conclude?** (APFC 2023 favourite Q.) Conciliation under Sec 12 *deemed* to conclude when:
- A settlement is arrived at and signed, OR
- A failure report is sent to the appropriate government, OR
- The reference is withdrawn, OR
- The dispute is referred to adjudication.
What is NOT a ground for conclusion — *expiry of a time period* alone (conciliation has *no* statutory time limit; the trap option will say "after 30 days" or "after 14 days").

## 2.5 The IR Code 2020 shifts (memorise these — they are the *change* questions)

The Industrial Relations Code 2020 **subsumed** IDA 1947, TU Act 1926 and Standing Orders Act 1946. The **headline changes** the exam tests:

| Old position | IR Code 2020 position |
|---|---|
| Standing Orders apply at 100+ workmen | **300+ workmen** |
| Lay-off / retrenchment / closure govt permission at 100+ | **300+ workmen** (states can lower) |
| Multiple definitions of "worker" | Single unified definition; wage threshold ₹18,000 (vs ₹10,000 in IDA) |
| Multiple tribunals (LC, IT, NT) | Single **Industrial Tribunal** (2-member: judicial + administrative) |
| Strike notice in any industry — none mandatory | **60 days strike notice mandatory** in ALL industries (was only for public utility under IDA) |
| Trade unions could be registered but had no "recognition" framework | New concept of **Negotiating Union** / **Negotiating Council** for collective bargaining |
| Re-skilling fund | New **Worker Re-skilling Fund** (Sec 83) for retrenched workers |

## 2.6 How these are tested

**Pattern A — "Which is NOT a public utility service under IDA First Schedule?"** Always answer by exclusion. Telegraph, telephone, postal, railways, supply of power/light/water to the public, public conservancy/sanitation — these ARE public utilities. Banking is public utility in *some* states only. *Education, insurance, road transport* are typically NOT, unless notified.

**Pattern B — "What is the minimum membership for trade union registration?"** **7 workmen** to apply; **at least 10% of workforce or 100 workmen, whichever is less** to sustain. State both numbers; the exam wants the right framing.

**Pattern C — Chronological order Q.** Memorise: AITUC 1920, INTUC 1947, HMS 1948, UTUC 1949, BMS 1955, CITU 1970, SEWA 1972.

**Pattern D — Match the Following (Federation ↔ Party).** AITUC-CPI, INTUC-Congress, HMS-Socialist (independent), CITU-CPM, BMS-BJP, UTUC-RSP.

**Pattern E — Lay-off vs retrenchment vs closure.** Identify which one applies; pick the matching threshold and compensation.

**Pattern F — Statement Q on Sec 9C / Sec 36(4) / Sec 12.** Recall the specific rule (20+ workmen for GRC; both consent + leave for legal rep; conciliation has no time-bar).

**Pattern G — IR Code change Q.** "Under the new IR Code, the standing-orders threshold is..." — answer 300.

## 2.7 Trap pairs

| The pair | The cue |
|---|---|
| Trade dispute (TU Act) vs Industrial dispute (IDA) | Same words; **TU Act for union rights, IDA for adjudication** |
| Lay-off vs Retrenchment vs Closure | Lay-off temporary; retrenchment individual termination; closure whole shop |
| Conciliation Officer vs Labour Court | Conciliation mediates (Sec 4); Labour Court *adjudicates* (Sec 7 — Second Schedule matters) |
| Labour Court vs Industrial Tribunal | LC = Second Schedule (dismissal, discipline). IT = Third Schedule (wages, bonus, hours) |
| Sec 25F (retrenchment) vs Sec 25-O (closure) | Both need notice + compensation; closure additionally needs govt permission if 100+ workmen |
| BMS vs INTUC | BMS = RSS/BJP, founded 1955 (now largest). INTUC = Congress, founded 1947 |
| 6-week notice (IDA, public utility) vs 60-day notice (IR Code, all industries) | OLD law = 6 weeks in PU only. NEW Code = 60 days everywhere |

## 2.8 Self-test

1. A factory of 250 workmen wants to lay off 30 workers due to power shortage. Under the *old* IDA, does the employer need government permission? Under the *IR Code 2020*?
2. Three workers register a trade union with just themselves. Is it valid?
3. Under IDA, a dispute reaches conciliation on 1 March. By 30 April there is no settlement. The Conciliation Officer says "since 60 days have passed, conciliation is over." Correct?
4. AITUC, INTUC, BMS, CITU — chronological order of founding?
5. Mr X, a foreman drawing ₹15,000/month, is dismissed. Can he file an IDA claim as a "workman"?

**Answers:**

1. Under *old* IDA: yes, permission needed (100+ workmen threshold). Under IR Code 2020: no (threshold is 300; only 250 here). This is the *exact* lubricant of the new Code's flexibility argument.
2. No — minimum **seven** workmen must subscribe to the rules to apply for registration.
3. No — conciliation under Sec 12 has **no statutory time limit**. It concludes only on settlement / failure report / withdrawal / reference to adjudication.
4. AITUC (1920) → INTUC (1947) → BMS (1955) → CITU (1970).
5. Yes, under the IDA: the wage threshold for exclusion from "workman" is ₹10,000/month *only for those in managerial/administrative capacity*. A foreman doing supervisory work is included up to a wage threshold; check whether his work is mainly supervisory and whether he draws above ₹10,000 doing *managerial* work. If purely supervisory (not managerial), he counts as workman regardless. The trap option will say "he is not a workman because he draws ₹15,000".

## 2.9 ILC — Indian Labour Conference (High-Yield Tripartite Body)

**Indian Labour Conference (ILC)** is the **apex tripartite body** in India's industrial relations framework.

| Feature | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| Composition | Tripartite: **Government** + **Employers** (CII, FICCI, ASSOCHAM, etc.) + **Workers** (central trade union federations) |
| Purpose | Discuss major national labour policy issues; reach consensus on wage norms, labour laws, working conditions |
| Status | Non-statutory; decisions are **advisory/non-binding** on the government |
| Standing Labour Committee (SLC) | Sub-body of ILC; meets more frequently to review implementation and prepare agenda for ILC |
| Exam question | "Which is the highest tripartite body for industrial relations in India?" → **ILC** |

<div class="examtip">

⚠️ **TRAP — ILC decisions are NON-BINDING.** ILC is consultative — its recommendations guide policy but do not have legal force. Candidates sometimes assume that ILC resolutions are automatically enforceable. They are not. The government must legislate or notify separately.

</div>

# CHAPTER 3 — The Wages-Welfare Pentad

> **What you must know after reading this chapter:** the five laws that protect a worker's *take-home money + sickness/injury/maternity/retirement cushion* — Payment of Wages (1936), Minimum Wages (1948), Payment of Bonus (1965), Payment of Gratuity (1972), Maternity Benefit (1961) — and the three companion laws (ESI 1948, Employees' Compensation 1923, BOCW 1996) that fill specific gaps. You should be able to compute any **threshold, eligibility, quantum, or time-limit** under each.

## 3.0 The 60-second picture

Think of a worker's pay-cheque. The State has placed protections around it at every point of vulnerability:
- **Before the cheque is cut**, the State sets a **floor wage** — *Minimum Wages Act 1948*.
- **At the moment of payment**, the State controls **when and how** the wages reach the worker, and what may be **deducted** — *Payment of Wages Act 1936*.
- **At year-end profit-sharing**, the State mandates a minimum **bonus** — *Payment of Bonus Act 1965*.
- **At separation** (retirement, resignation after 5 yrs, death), the State mandates a **gratuity** — *Payment of Gratuity Act 1972*.
- **During pregnancy/childbirth**, the State mandates **paid maternity leave** — *Maternity Benefit Act 1961*.

And three companions for specific shocks:
- **Sickness/injury/medical care** — *ESI Act 1948* (contributory insurance).
- **Industrial accident/disease** — *Employees' Compensation Act 1923* (employer pays compensation; no insurance involved).
- **Construction worker welfare** — *BOCW Act 1996* + Cess Act (welfare board funded by 1% cess on construction cost).

All of these — except EC 1923 — are now subsumed under the **Code on Wages 2019** (first four) and **Code on Social Security 2020** (the rest). But the underlying numbers and concepts are still the exam's bread and butter.

## 3.2 Law-by-law — the facts you actually need

### Payment of Wages Act, 1936

**The purpose.** Regulate **when** wages are paid and what can be **deducted**.

**Coverage.** All workers in scheduled industrial establishments drawing wages up to **₹24,000 per month** (this ceiling, raised in 2017 from ₹18,000, is the live number).

**Wage-period.** Cannot exceed **one month**.

**Timing of payment.** 
- Establishments with **fewer than 1,000 workers** — pay by the **7th** of the next month.
- Establishments with **1,000 or more workers** — pay by the **10th** of the next month.

**Mode of payment.** Current coin, currency notes, cheque, or direct bank credit. (Compulsory bank credit since 2017 amendment for certain establishments.)

**Permissible deductions (Sec 7).** Limited list:
- Fines (subject to Sec 8 limits — max 3% of wages, employee must be heard)
- Absence from duty
- Damage / loss
- House accommodation, amenities, services *if accepted by worker*
- Recovery of advances or loans
- Payments to co-operative societies or PF
- Income-tax / authorised deductions to govt
**Total deductions max:** 50% of wages (75% if cooperative society deductions are involved).

**Registers & records (Sec 13A + Rule 5).** Every employer must maintain registers of: wages paid, fines imposed, deductions made, advances given. **Preserve for 3 years** from last entry.

**Authority for claims.** Aggrieved worker files claim before the **Authority appointed under Sec 15** (an officer not below the rank of Presiding Officer of Labour Court). Application within **12 months** (extendable on sufficient cause).

### Minimum Wages Act, 1948

**The purpose.** Ensure no worker is paid below a State-fixed floor.

**The two big concepts:**
- **Time-rate** and **piece-rate** minimum wages — the Act allows both.
- **Guaranteed time-rate** = a minimum-time-basis wage notionally applied to a piece-worker so even slow piece-workers earn a floor. (EO/AO 2023 exact term.)

**Review and revision.** The appropriate government must review/revise minimum wages **at intervals not exceeding 5 years** (Sec 3(1)(b)). This is the favourite recall fact.

**Components of minimum wage** (the 1948 Tripartite Committee + later judicial decisions like *Workmen of Reptakos Brett v Their Management* 1992):
1. Three consumption units per earner
2. 2,700 calories/day per adult
3. Cloth requirement 18 yards/year per adult
4. Rent equivalent to govt scheme rent
5. Fuel + lighting + misc = 20% of minimum wage
6. Children's education + medical + recreation = additional 25%

**Categories of workers** (Sec 2): unskilled, semi-skilled, skilled, highly-skilled — different minimum wages for each.

**Subsumed by Code on Wages 2019.** The Code introduces a **Floor Wage** to be set by the Central Government (advised by the Central Advisory Board) which states cannot go below.

### Code on Wages, 2019 — Key Definitions (Exam-Favourite)

The Code on Wages 2019 consolidated four laws: **Payment of Wages Act 1936, Minimum Wages Act 1948, Payment of Bonus Act 1965, and Equal Remuneration Act 1976** into a single Code.

| Term | Definition under the Code | Exam point |
|------|--------------------------|-----------|
| **Floor Wage** | Minimum base wage set by the **Central Government** after consulting Central Advisory Board; **no state can fix a minimum wage BELOW the floor wage** | New concept introduced by the Code; distinguishes from state minimum wage |
| **Minimum Wage** | Wage fixed by the **state/appropriate government** for different scheduled employments; must be at or above the floor wage | Different states can set different rates above the floor |
| **National Minimum Wage** | Advisory concept (NITI Aayog debates ₹178/day or higher); **not legally binding** yet | Debate ongoing; not same as floor wage |
| **Equal Remuneration** | Same pay for same work regardless of gender; Code incorporates the **Equal Remuneration Act 1976** into Chapter IV | "Equal pay for equal work" made part of the Code |
| **Worker** | The Code uses a single unified definition covering manual, unskilled, skilled, technical, operational, clerical, or supervisory work (with monetary threshold: wage ≤ ₹18,000/month for some provisions) | Replaces four different definitions from the four old Acts |
| **Advisory Board** | Central Advisory Board (advises on floor wage); State Advisory Boards (advise on state minimum wages); tripartite composition | Tests who advises vs who decides |

<div class="keypoint">

**Labour Codes implementation status (as of 2026):** All four Labour Codes (Wages 2019, Industrial Relations 2020, Social Security 2020, OSH 2020) were passed by Parliament. However, **implementation has been deferred** — most states have not yet notified their rules under the Codes, so the **old Acts continue to govern in practice**. Examiners test BOTH: "what does the old law say?" AND "what will the Code change?" Read questions carefully for the context (current law vs future Code).

</div>

### Payment of Bonus Act, 1965

**The purpose.** Statutorily share a slice of the employer's profit (or productivity) with workers.

**Eligibility (Sec 2(13) + Sec 8).** Employee drawing wages **up to ₹21,000/month** (raised from ₹10,000 in 2015). Must have worked at least **30 days** in the accounting year. Establishment with **20+ employees** (10+ if notified).

**Minimum bonus (Sec 10).** **8.33% of salary/wage** or **₹100**, whichever is higher — payable *even if there is no profit* (this is the "minimum bonus" guarantee).

**Maximum bonus (Sec 11).** **20% of salary/wage**, payable when allocable surplus exceeds the minimum bonus payable.

**The wage cap for calculation (Sec 12).** Even if the employee's wage exceeds ₹7,000 (or the minimum wage for the relevant scheduled employment, whichever is higher), **bonus is computed as if wage = ₹7,000 or the minimum wage, whichever is higher**.

**Worked example (APFC 2023 favourite):**
- Employee draws ₹9,000/month under Minimum Wages Act.
- Eligibility for bonus under PoB Act 2021-22: wage ≤ ₹21,000 ✓.
- Bonus computed on ₹9,000 (because the minimum-wages figure is higher than the ₹7,000 cap).
- Minimum bonus = 8.33% × 9,000 × 12 = **₹8,996.40** per year.

**Time-limit for payment (Sec 19).** Within **8 months** from close of the accounting year; extendable to 2 years with government permission.

### Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972

**The purpose.** Lump-sum reward at separation, for **long-service workers**.

**Coverage.** Every establishment with **10 or more employees** at any time in the preceding 12 months (sticky once covered). All sectors — factory, mine, plantation, shop, govt, NGO.

**Eligibility (Sec 4(1)).** **5 years of continuous service** required, EXCEPT in case of **death or disablement** — where the 5-year condition is waived.

**Formula.**
$$\text{Gratuity} = \frac{15 \times \text{Last drawn wage} \times \text{Yrs of service}}{26}$$
- *15* = 15 days' wage per year
- *26* = number of working days in a month (excluding 4 Sundays)
- Service of > 6 months in the last year counts as a full year (Sec 4(2) rule)

**For employees not covered by the Act** (private gratuity scheme), the formula uses 30 in the denominator and basic+DA in the numerator, and the days = 15 days for full year.

**Maximum gratuity payable** (Sec 4(3)): **₹20 lakh** (raised from ₹10 lakh by the 2018 amendment; this number is exam-favourite).

**Forfeiture of gratuity (Sec 4(6)).** Allowed only if termination is for proven misconduct involving riot, violence, moral turpitude — and partial forfeiture for damage caused to employer.

**Time-limit for payment.** Within **30 days** of becoming payable; otherwise interest payable.

**Penalty for non-payment.** Imprisonment up to 1 year and/or fine up to ₹20,000 (Sec 9).

### Maternity Benefit Act, 1961 (as amended 2017)

**The purpose.** Paid maternity leave + workplace accommodations for working women.

**Coverage.** Every establishment (factory/mine/plantation/shop) with **10+ employees** where the woman has worked at least **80 days** in the 12 months preceding her expected date of delivery.

**Maternity benefit quantum (Sec 5, post-2017 amendment).**
- **26 weeks** of paid leave — for women with **fewer than 2 surviving children**.
- **12 weeks** — for women with **2 or more surviving children**.
- **12 weeks** — for women adopting a child below 3 months of age, or commissioning mothers (surrogacy).

**Distribution of leave.** Up to **8 weeks before** expected delivery + remainder after. (Pre-natal cap is 8 weeks; post-natal makes up the balance.)

**Crèche facility (Sec 11A — inserted 2017).** Every establishment with **50+ employees** shall have a crèche facility within prescribed distance. Mother allowed **4 visits** per day to the crèche (including rest intervals).

**Nursing breaks (Sec 11).** Two breaks of prescribed duration, in addition to interval for rest, until child is **15 months old**.

**Work-from-home option** (Sec 5(5), 2017 amendment). Employer *may* permit WFH if nature of work permits, after maternity benefit period ends.

**Government servants — exam catch.** Maternity benefit of **26 weeks** under MBA applies to women in *establishments to which the Act applies*. Govt servants are governed by **Central Civil Services (Leave) Rules** which already provide 180 days = ~26 weeks. The exam Q on "circumstances when Ms X, a government servant, can claim 26 weeks under MBA 1961" is a trick — strictly speaking the MBA does not apply to govt servants unless their service rules incorporate it; the safe answer is: *for the birth of the first or second surviving child*.

**Nursing-break duration (Sec 11 — APFC 2025 Q).** Two breaks of prescribed duration, in addition to interval for rest. **Until child is 15 months old.**

### Employees' State Insurance Act, 1948

**The purpose.** Contributory insurance for sickness, maternity, disablement, dependants' benefit, medical care.

**Wage ceiling.** **₹21,000/month** (₹25,000 for persons with disability).

**Contribution rates** (since 1 July 2019):
- Employee: **0.75%** of wages
- Employer: **3.25%** of wages
- **Total: 4.00%**

(Older rates were 1.75% + 4.75% = 6.5%; the 2019 reduction stuck.)

**Worked example.** Wage ₹20,000. Employee contributes 0.75% × 20,000 = **₹150**; Employer contributes 3.25% × 20,000 = **₹650**. (APFC 2025 Q.)

**Benefits (6 types).** Sickness, maternity, disablement (temp/permanent), dependants', medical, funeral. Sickness benefit = **70% of wages** for 91 days/year max.

**SPREE 2025** (Scheme for Promotion of Registration of Employers and Employees) — relief scheme allowing past defaulters to register without prosecution; approved by **ESIC** (not EPFO — common trap), period: typically a 6-month window.

### Employees' Compensation Act, 1923 (renamed from Workmen's Compensation Act in 2010)

**The purpose.** Compensation by the **employer** to a workman injured/killed in employment — *no insurance involved*, employer pays directly.

**Coverage.** All workers in scheduled employments (Schedule II includes ~50 categories — factory workers, miners, plantation, transport, etc.). **No wage ceiling.**

**Schedule I — list of injuries and percentage of permanent disablement.** This is the most asked Schedule:
- Loss of right arm above elbow: **70%**
- Loss of right arm at or below elbow: **60%**
- Loss of left arm above elbow: **60%**
- Loss of left arm at or below elbow: **50%**
- Loss of one foot: **40%**
- Loss of sight of one eye **(without complication, other eye normal)**: **30%** — this is **partial disablement, not total** (APFC 2025 exact framing of the Q).
- Loss of hearing both ears: **50%** (partial of total).

**Compensation formula (Sec 4):**
- **Death**: 50% of monthly wages × relevant factor (from Schedule IV), or **₹1,20,000**, whichever is more.
- **Permanent total disablement**: 60% of monthly wages × relevant factor, or **₹1,40,000**, whichever is more.
- **Permanent partial disablement**: % of the Sched I disablement × the permanent-total figure.
- **Temporary disablement**: 25% of wages, half-monthly, during the disablement.

**Worked example (APFC 2025 Q).** Person X, age 48, mine worker, monthly wage ₹12,000, fatal accident.
- Relevant factor for age 48 (from Sched IV): approximately **169.44**.
- Compensation = 50% × 12,000 × 169.44 = ₹10,16,640. Compared with floor of ₹1,20,000 — higher of the two = **₹10,16,640**.

**Reference time-limit (Sec 22A — APFC 2023 Q).** Time within which Commissioner shall pass an order on a reference: prescribed by State rules — **typically 3 months**.

### Building & Other Construction Workers Act, 1996 + BOCW Cess Act, 1996

**The purpose.** Welfare of unorganised construction workers via a State-level Welfare Board funded by a cess on construction cost.

**Cess rate.** **1% to 2%** of the cost of construction (typically 1% notified by the Centre).

**Welfare Board benefits.** Pension, accident assistance, maternity, education for children, housing, marriage, funeral, medical, skill-training.

**Mandatory registration.** Every employer of 10+ construction workers must register the establishment; every construction worker aged 18-60 having 90+ days of work in the preceding 12 months can register as a beneficiary.

### Inter-State Migrant Workmen Act, 1979 (now subsumed in OSH Code 2020)

**The purpose.** Protect workers recruited in one state for work in another (via a *contractor*).

**Key facts.**
- Applies to establishments employing **5 or more** inter-state migrant workmen via a contractor (separate from contract labour threshold of 20+ in CLRA).
- **Registration** by principal employer; **licensing** by contractor.
- **Displacement allowance** — 50% of monthly wages or ₹75, whichever higher.
- **Journey allowance** — to/from home state.

### Mines Act, 1952

**Salient features.**
- Applies to all mines (coal, metal, oil-well, stone-quarry above prescribed threshold).
- **Adult worker** max hours: 8/day (10/day above ground), 48/week.
- **Weekly rest day** mandatory.
- **Crèche** at 50+ women workers (same threshold as MBA).
- **Special Officer** of the Mines Department can enter a mine for **survey, levelling, measuring** after giving **prescribed notice** to the mine owner (APFC 2023 Q).

| MBA | 10+ employees, 80+ days of work in last 12 months |

| EC Act | all scheduled employments — no wage ceiling |
| BOCW Act | 10+ construction workers |
| ISMW Act | 5+ inter-state migrant workmen via contractor |

## 3.4 How these are tested

**Pattern A — Direct threshold recall.** "What is the maximum gratuity payable under the PoG Act?" → ₹20 lakh.

**Pattern B — Worked numerical.** Wage × percentage × period. Always check whether the wage exceeds a ceiling (ESI cap ₹21K, EPS cap ₹15K, PoB calc cap ₹7K-or-MW).

**Pattern C — Eligibility scenario.** "Mr X resigns after 6 years. Eligible for gratuity?" Yes (≥ 5 yrs). "Mr Y dies after 3 years in service. Eligible?" Yes (5-yr rule waived on death).

**Pattern D — Statement 1/2/3 multi-correct.** PoW Act, MBA — "Which of the following are correct?" Resolve each statement against the section facts above.

**Pattern E — Sched I disablement %.** Loss of one eye = 30%; loss of right arm above elbow = 70%; loss of left arm below = 50%. Memorise the top 5 numbers.

**Pattern F — Match the Following.** Common pairs: PoW Act ↔ wage protection; MW Act ↔ floor; PoB ↔ profit-share; PoG ↔ exit reward; MBA ↔ maternity; ESI ↔ medical; EC ↔ accident.

## 3.5 Trap pairs

| The pair | The cue |
|---|---|
| PoW (₹24K ceiling) vs ESI (₹21K ceiling) vs PoB (₹21K ceiling) vs EPS (₹15K calc cap) | Different numbers; memorise as **24-21-21-15** |
| Gratuity calc denominator: 26 vs 30 | **26** for covered (the Act assumes 4 Sundays off); 30 for uncovered employees |
| Maternity 26 weeks vs 12 weeks | 26 for first/second child; 12 for third+ |
| Maternity crèche threshold (50) vs Factories Act crèche threshold (30 women) | MBA at 50 *employees* (any gender); Factories at 30 *women* workers |
| ESI rate (0.75% + 3.25%) vs EPF rate (12% + 12%) | ESI is *insurance*; EPF is *savings* — that's why ESI is so much smaller |
| EC Act (loss of one eye = 30%) — partial, not total | Other-eye-normal scenario, hence partial |
| Sched I (EC Act disablement list) vs Sched II (EC Act covered employments list) | Always say *Schedule I = body parts %, Schedule II = jobs covered* |
| MW review interval (5 yrs) vs Bonus payment limit (8 months) | Both are time-bound but unrelated; don't swap them |

## 3.6 Self-test

1. A factory of 25 workers pays them on the **15th** of next month. Is this legal under PoW Act?
2. Ms A has worked 75 days in last 12 months. Eligible for maternity benefit?
3. Mr B's monthly wage = ₹6,000 under MW Act. Compute his minimum annual bonus.
4. Mr C, age 40, mine worker, monthly wage ₹15,000, fatal accident. Compute compensation under EC Act (relevant factor 184.17).
5. A worker loses sight in his left eye (right eye normal). What % disablement under EC Act Schedule I?

**Answers:**
1. No — PoW Act requires payment by the **7th** of next month for establishments with fewer than 1,000 workers. 15th is illegal; claim under Sec 15 lies.
2. No — MBA requires **80 days** of work in the 12 months preceding expected delivery. 75 falls short.
3. ₹6,000 > ₹7,000? No. ₹6,000 < ₹7,000 → bonus computed on **₹7,000** (or MW, whichever higher; here MW is ₹6,000 so ₹7,000 is higher). Min bonus = 8.33% × 7,000 × 12 = **₹6,997.20** per year.
4. 50% × 15,000 × 184.17 = ₹13,81,275. Floor ₹1,20,000 — higher of two = **₹13,81,275**.
5. **30%** partial disablement (loss of one eye without complication, other eye normal — Schedule I).

## 3.7 One sentence to carry forever

> *Five protections wrap the worker's pay-cheque — a floor, timely payment, a profit slice, an exit reward, a maternity cushion — and three companions catch the rest: medical (ESI), accident (EC), and the construction underclass (BOCW).*

# CHAPTER 4 — The Constitution-Labour Bridge

## 4.0 The 60-second picture

Every labour law you learned in Chapters 1-3 hangs from a hook in the Constitution. The hook is **Article 246 + Schedule 7**, which decides *who can make the law* (Centre or State). The *reason* the law exists is in the **Directive Principles** (Articles 39, 41, 42, 43, 43A) — the promises the State made to workers in 1950. The *limits* on those laws are in the **Fundamental Rights** (Articles 14, 19, 21, 23, 24). So labour law sits inside a triangle: **Power (who legislates) → Purpose (why) → Protection (what can't be violated).** Learn the triangle and you can place any new labour question instantly.

## 4.1 The mental model — the three-floor building

Picture a three-floor building. Labour law lives on the ground floor where the actual work happens. But it is held up by two floors above it.

```
   ┌──────────────────────────────────────────┐
   │  FLOOR 3: FUNDAMENTAL RIGHTS (the ceiling)  │
   │  Art 14 equality · 19(1)(c) form unions ·   │
   │  21 life+livelihood · 23 no forced labour · │
   │  24 no child <14 in hazardous work          │
   │  → These set the CEILING. No law may pierce │
   ├──────────────────────────────────────────┤
   │  FLOOR 2: DIRECTIVE PRINCIPLES (the mandate) │
   │  Art 39(a/d/e) · 41 right to work · 42 just  │
   │  & humane conditions+maternity relief ·      │
   │  43 living wage · 43A worker participation   │
   │  → These are the REASON the floor exists.    │
   │     Non-justiciable but "fundamental in      │
   │     governance" (Art 37)                     │
   ├──────────────────────────────────────────┤
   │  FLOOR 1: THE LABOUR CODES (the workshop)    │
   │  Wages · IR · OSH · Social Security          │
   │  Made under Art 246 + Schedule 7 List III    │
   │  (Concurrent) — both Centre & State act      │
   └──────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

The exam loves to test the **boundary lines** between the floors: "Is the right to form a union a Fundamental Right or a labour-law right?" (Answer: it is **both** — Art 19(1)(c) is the FR ceiling; the Trade Unions Act is the ground-floor mechanism.) "Is the right to a living wage enforceable in court?" (No — it is a Directive Principle, Art 43, non-justiciable.)

## 4.2 The story

In 1950 the framers faced a contradiction. They wanted to *guarantee* workers a living wage, decent hours and dignity — but a poor, newly-independent country could not afford to make those guarantees enforceable overnight. So they split the promises into two boxes. The promises the State **must** keep immediately (no forced labour, no child labour in mines, equality before law) went into **Part III — Fundamental Rights**, enforceable by the courts under Art 32. The promises the State would keep **as resources allowed** (living wage, work participation, humane conditions) went into **Part IV — Directive Principles**, declared "fundamental in the governance of the country" (Art 37) but explicitly *not* enforceable by any court.

Over seventy years the Supreme Court quietly demolished the wall between the two boxes. In *Bandhua Mukti Morcha* (1984) it read the Directive Principle of humane conditions **into** Article 21's "right to life," making bonded-labour rescue enforceable. In *Olga Tellis* (1985) it held the right to livelihood is part of the right to life. So today a clever lawyer turns an unenforceable Directive Principle into an enforceable Fundamental Right by routing it through Article 21. That judicial bridge — DPSP read into Art 21 — is the single most tested idea in this chapter.

## 4.3 The facts you actually need

**Article 246 + Seventh Schedule — who legislates.** Three lists:

| List | Who legislates | Labour-relevant entries |
|---|---|---|
| **List I — Union** | Centre only | Entry 55 (regulation of labour & safety in mines and oilfields), Entry 61 (industrial disputes concerning Union employees) |
| **List II — State** | State only | (residual local matters) |
| **List III — Concurrent** | Both; Centre prevails on conflict (Art 254) | **Entry 22** trade unions, industrial & labour disputes; **Entry 23** social security & social insurance, EPF, employment & unemployment; **Entry 24** welfare of labour, conditions of work, provident funds, employers' liability, ESI |

> **The single fact examiners ask most:** Labour is in the **Concurrent List** (Entries 22, 23, 24). That is *why* both Parliament and State Assemblies can pass labour laws, and why the four Labour Codes needed careful Centre-State coordination.

**The Directive Principles that mention labour (Part IV):**

| Article | Promise |
|---|---|
| **39(a)** | Adequate means of livelihood for all citizens |
| **39(d)** | **Equal pay for equal work** for men and women |
| **39(e)** | Health & strength of workers not abused; not forced by economic necessity into unsuitable work |
| **41** | Right to work, to education, to public assistance (old age, sickness, unemployment) |
| **42** | Just and humane conditions of work + **maternity relief** |
| **43** | **Living wage**, decent standard of life, leisure; promotion of cottage industries |
| **43A** | **Participation of workers in management** (added by 42nd Amendment, 1976) |
| **43B** | Promotion of co-operative societies (97th Amendment, 2011) |

**The Fundamental Rights that cap labour law (Part III):**

| Article | Right | Labour link |
|---|---|---|
| **14** | Equality before law / equal protection | Basis for equal-pay & anti-discrimination cases |
| **19(1)(c)** | Freedom to form **associations or unions** | Constitutional root of the Trade Unions Act |
| **21** | Protection of life & personal liberty | Expanded to include livelihood, humane conditions, health |
| **23** | Prohibition of **traffic in humans & forced labour** (*begar*) | Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976 |
| **24** | No **child below 14** in any factory, mine or hazardous employment | Child & Adolescent Labour (Prohibition) Act |

**Wage / minimum-wage in the Constitution:** there is **no Fundamental Right to a minimum wage as such**, but the Supreme Court in *People's Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India* (1982, the Asiad Workers case) held that **paying less than the minimum wage = forced labour under Article 23**, because a person who works below subsistence is "forced" by economic compulsion. This converts a wage question into a Fundamental-Rights question — a favourite trap.

## 4.4 How these are tested

- **Which list?** "Trade unions fall under which list of the Seventh Schedule?" → Concurrent (Entry 22).
- **FR vs DPSP sorting.** "Equal pay for equal work is a — (a) Fundamental Right  (b) Directive Principle  (c) Fundamental Duty." → **Directive Principle, Art 39(d)** — but note it has been *enforced* via Art 14+16, so read the question carefully.
- **Which Amendment added it?** Art 43A (workers in management) → **42nd Amendment 1976**; Art 43B (co-operatives) → 97th Amendment 2011.
- **Case-to-principle.** "Non-payment of minimum wage was held to be forced labour in which case?" → *PUDR v. UoI* (Asiad Workers, 1982).
- **Which Article protects children?** Art 24 (below 14, hazardous). Art 21A (right to education 6-14) is the companion.

## 4.5 Trap pairs

- **Art 23 vs Art 24** — 23 = forced labour & trafficking (all ages); 24 = child labour below 14 in hazardous work. Don't swap.
- **Art 39(d) vs Art 14** — 39(d) is the *Directive Principle* of equal pay; Art 14 is the *Fundamental Right* of equality used to enforce it.
- **Living wage vs minimum wage vs fair wage** — *Living wage* (Art 43, the aspiration) > *Fair wage* > *Minimum wage* (the statutory floor, enforceable). Only the **minimum wage** is legally enforceable; the living wage is a Directive Principle goal.
- **42nd vs 44th Amendment** — 42nd (1976) added 43A, the words "Socialist Secular," and Fundamental Duties; 44th (1978) *removed* the right to property from FRs. Workers' participation = 42nd.
- **DPSP non-justiciable, but...** — the *rule* (Art 37) is they cannot be enforced in court; the *exception* is when a court reads them into Art 21. Both halves get tested.

## 4.6 Self-test (60 seconds each)

1. Under which Schedule and List does "social security and social insurance" fall?
2. Name the Article that guarantees the freedom to form unions and the Article (DPSP) that promotes worker participation in management.
3. Which case held that paying below minimum wage amounts to forced labour, and under which Article?
4. Is "equal pay for equal work" a Fundamental Right or a Directive Principle? What is the Article number?
5. Which Constitutional Amendment inserted Article 43A?

**Answers:** 1) Seventh Schedule, **Concurrent List, Entry 23**. 2) Form unions = **Art 19(1)(c)** (FR); worker participation = **Art 43A** (DPSP). 3) *PUDR v. Union of India* (Asiad Workers, 1982), under **Article 23**. 4) **Directive Principle, Article 39(d)** (enforced through Art 14/16). 5) **42nd Amendment, 1976.**

## 4.7 One sentence to carry forever

> **Labour law is born in the Concurrent List (power), justified by the Directive Principles (purpose), and capped by Fundamental Rights (protection) — and the Supreme Court's master-key is reading any Directive Principle into Article 21 to make it enforceable.**

# CHAPTER 5 — Polity Beyond Labour

## 5.0 The 60-second picture

The APFC paper carries ~28 marks of pure Constitution/Polity beyond the labour bridge. It is the *cheapest* mark per hour in the whole syllabus because it is finite and factual. Picture the Indian State as **three organs (Legislature, Executive, Judiciary)** kept honest by **four watchdog bodies (EC, CAG, UPSC, Finance Commission + AG)**, all operating under **one rulebook that can be amended (Art 368)** but not in its **basic structure**. Master five things — how Parliament works, how a bill becomes law, who guards the Constitution, how it is amended, and the emergency switches — and you bank these marks.

## 5.1 The mental model — government as a relay race

Think of governance as a relay with a baton called *legitimacy*.

```
   PEOPLE
     │ (elect)
     ▼
   LOK SABHA (545) ── money bills start here, real power
     │  + RAJYA SABHA (245) ── states' house, never dissolved
     ▼ (a bill cleared by both + President's assent)
   LAW
     │ (executed by)
     ▼
   PRESIDENT → PM + Council of Ministers (real executive)
     │ (and checked by)
     ▼
   SUPREME COURT → guards the Constitution (judicial review)
```

The baton can only move if each runner follows the rules. The exam tests the *handoff points*: how many readings a bill needs, who can introduce a money bill, what happens in a joint sitting, who can declare an emergency, who interprets the Constitution.

## 5.2 Parliament — the facts you actually need

**Composition:**

| House | Strength (max) | Term | Presiding officer |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Lok Sabha** | 552 (530 states + 20 UTs + 2 Anglo-Indian *abolished by 104th Amdt 2019*) | 5 years (dissolvable) | Speaker |
| **Rajya Sabha** | 250 (238 elected + 12 nominated) | Permanent; 1/3 retire every 2 yrs (6-yr term) | Vice-President (ex-officio Chairman) |

**How a bill becomes law (ordinary bill):** Introduction (First Reading) → Second Reading (general discussion + clause-by-clause) → Third Reading (vote) → other House repeats → President's assent. **Money Bill (Art 110):** can be introduced **only in Lok Sabha**, only on President's recommendation; Rajya Sabha can only *recommend* changes within **14 days** and Lok Sabha may reject them — Speaker certifies a Money Bill and the decision is final.

**Joint sitting (Art 108):** called by President to resolve a deadlock on an *ordinary* bill (never a money bill or constitutional amendment); presided over by the **Speaker**; decided by simple majority of total members present. Only **three** joint sittings have ever happened (Dowry Prohibition 1961, Banking Service Commission Repeal 1978, **POTA 2002**).

**Key terms:** *Quorum* = 1/10 of total members. *Question Hour* (first hour), *Zero Hour* (immediately after, no notice). Types of majority: **Simple** (present & voting), **Absolute** (of total strength), **Effective** (total minus vacancies), **Special** (2/3 of present & voting + majority of total — used for amendments & impeachment).

## 5.3 The Judiciary & guardianship of the Constitution

- **Supreme Court (Art 124):** Chief Justice + up to **33** other judges (34 total). Judges retire at **65**; High Court judges at **62**.
- **Judicial Review** — the power to strike down laws violating the Constitution; rooted in Art 13.
- **Basic Structure Doctrine** — *Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973)*: Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution under Art 368 but **cannot destroy its "basic structure"** (rule of law, judicial review, federalism, secularism, free & fair elections). This single doctrine is tested almost every year.
- **Writs (Art 32 SC / Art 226 HC):** Habeas Corpus (produce the body), Mandamus (we command — to a public authority), Prohibition (stop, to a lower court), Certiorari (be certified — quash an order), Quo Warranto (by what authority — challenge a usurped office). Art 32 is itself a Fundamental Right — Ambedkar called it "the heart and soul of the Constitution."

## 5.4 Constitutional & statutory bodies — the watchdogs

| Body | Article | Appointed by | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Election Commission** | 324 | President | CEC + 2 ECs; removed like SC judge |
| **CAG** | 148 | President | Audits Union & State accounts; "guardian of the public purse"; 6-yr/65 |
| **UPSC** | 315-323 | President | Conducts central recruitment (incl. this very exam) |
| **Finance Commission** | 280 | President, every 5 yrs | Recommends tax devolution Centre→States |
| **Attorney General** | 76 | President | Highest law officer of the Union |
| **NITI Aayog** | (executive body, **no Article** — replaced Planning Commission 2015) | — | Think-tank, not constitutional |

> **Trap:** NITI Aayog and the Lokpal are **statutory/executive**, not constitutional. The Finance Commission and CAG **are** constitutional. Sorting "constitutional vs statutory vs non-statutory" is a stock question.

## 5.5 Amendments & Emergencies

**Amendment (Art 368)** — three routes: (a) simple majority (some provisions, outside 368), (b) special majority (most), (c) special majority **+ ratification by half the states** (federal provisions — election of President, distribution of powers, Schedule 7, representation of states, Art 368 itself).

**Emergencies — three types:**

| Type | Article | Ground | Notable |
|---|---|---|---|
| **National** | 352 | War, external aggression, **armed rebellion** | Declared 3 times: 1962, 1971, **1975-77** (internal). 44th Amdt changed "internal disturbance" → "armed rebellion" |
| **State (President's Rule)** | 356 | Failure of constitutional machinery in a State | *S.R. Bommai (1994)* made it judicially reviewable |
| **Financial** | 360 | Threat to financial stability/credit | **Never used** |

**Landmark amendments to remember:** 1st (1951, land reforms + 9th Schedule), 42nd (1976, "mini-constitution"), 44th (1978, undid Emergency excesses, removed right to property from FRs), 73rd & 74th (1992, Panchayats & Municipalities), 101st (2016, **GST**), 103rd (2019, 10% EWS reservation), 104th (2020, abolished Anglo-Indian nomination + extended SC/ST reservation).

## 5.6 How these are tested

- **Number facts:** SC judges (34), quorum (1/10), money-bill recommendation window (14 days), joint sittings held (3).
- **Article-to-body:** "CAG is under which Article?" → 148. "Finance Commission?" → 280.
- **Doctrine-to-case:** Basic structure → Kesavananda; President's Rule reviewable → Bommai.
- **Constitutional vs statutory:** NITI Aayog (no), Election Commission (yes).
- **Which majority:** Removal of a judge / amendment = special majority; ordinary bill = simple.

## 5.7 Trap pairs

- **Art 352 vs 356 vs 360** — National / State / Financial emergency. 360 never used.
- **Money Bill vs Finance Bill** — a Money Bill (Art 110) deals *only* with the matters listed there and Speaker certifies it; a Finance Bill is broader. All Money Bills are Finance Bills, not vice-versa.
- **Cut motions vs adjournment motion vs no-confidence** — different parliamentary devices; no-confidence is only against the entire Council of Ministers.
- **Planning Commission vs NITI Aayog vs Finance Commission** — first two are executive/non-constitutional; only the Finance Commission is in the Constitution (Art 280).
- **Speaker presides over joint sitting**, not the Vice-President (who chairs only the Rajya Sabha).

## 5.8 Self-test

1. In which House must a Money Bill be introduced, and how long can the other House hold it?
2. Name the case that established the Basic Structure Doctrine and the year.
3. Which Article covers President's Rule, and which case made it judicially reviewable?
4. Is the Finance Commission constitutional or statutory? Which Article?
5. How many joint sittings of Parliament have been held, and name the most recent law passed that way.

**Answers:** 1) **Lok Sabha**; Rajya Sabha may hold/recommend for **14 days** only. 2) ***Kesavananda Bharati*, 1973.** 3) **Article 356**; *S.R. Bommai v. Union of India*, 1994. 4) **Constitutional, Article 280.** 5) **Three**; most recent = **POTA, 2002.**

## 5.9 One sentence to carry forever

> **Three organs pass the baton of legitimacy under one amendable rulebook whose basic structure even Parliament cannot touch — and the watchdogs (EC, CAG, UPSC, Finance Commission) are the referees who keep the relay honest.**

# CHAPTER 6 — The Indian Economy Story

## 6.0 The 60-second picture

Economics on this paper is not a formula sheet — it is one **circular flow of money** with four valves the government turns: **what the country earns (National Income), how much money exists (Monetary policy), how the government spends & taxes (Fiscal policy/Budget), and how prices behave (Inflation).** Plus a fifth window — **how India trades with the world (BoP & FTAs).** Hold the circular flow in your head and every term — GDP, repo rate, fiscal deficit, CPI, current account — drops into its slot.

## 6.1 The mental model — the money merry-go-round

```
        ┌──────────────► HOUSEHOLDS ◄──────────────┐
        │  (supply labour, earn income, spend)       │
        │                    │ spend                  │ wages
        │ taxes              ▼                         │
   GOVERNMENT ◄── taxes ── FIRMS ── produce GDP ──────┘
        │  (spends = fiscal policy)
        ▼
   RBI turns the MONEY valve (repo rate, CRR, SLR)
        │
        ▼
   Too much money chasing goods → INFLATION
        │
        ▼
   Trade with WORLD → Balance of Payments (Current + Capital a/c)
```

Money goes round and round; the government and RBI only adjust the *speed* of the merry-go-round. Fiscal policy (Budget) is the **government's** hand; monetary policy (repo rate) is the **RBI's** hand. Inflation is what happens when the ride spins too fast.

## 6.2 National Income — what the country earns

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **GDP** | Value of all final goods & services produced **within the country** in a year |
| **GNP** | GDP + net factor income from abroad |
| **NNP** | GNP − depreciation |
| **NNP at factor cost = National Income** | NNP at market price − indirect taxes + subsidies |
| **Per Capita Income** | National Income ÷ population |
| **Nominal vs Real GDP** | Nominal = current prices; **Real = constant (base-year) prices**, strips out inflation |
| **GDP Deflator** | (Nominal ÷ Real GDP) × 100 — broadest inflation measure |

**Three methods to measure:** Production (value-added), Income (wages+rent+interest+profit), Expenditure (C+I+G+(X−M)). India's base year for GDP is currently **2022-23** (new series released by MoSPI in February 2026; replaced the old 2011-12 base); data is released by the **NSO (National Statistical Office)** under MoSPI. Sectors: Primary (agri), Secondary (industry), Tertiary (services — the largest share of India's GDP, ~50%+).

## 6.3 Money & Monetary Policy — the RBI's valve

**The RBI's toolkit (quantitative):**

| Tool | What it is | Raise it to... |
|---|---|---|
| **Repo rate** | Rate RBI lends to banks (against securities) | fight inflation (makes loans dearer) |
| **Reverse repo** | Rate RBI borrows from banks | absorb liquidity |
| **CRR** | % of deposits banks park with RBI (cash, no interest) | suck out money |
| **SLR** | % of deposits banks hold in liquid assets (gold, G-secs) | restrict credit |
| **MSF** | Marginal Standing Facility — emergency overnight borrowing, above repo | — |
| **Bank Rate** | Long-term lending rate (now aligned with MSF) | — |

**Monetary Policy Committee (MPC):** 6 members (3 RBI incl. Governor + 3 government-appointed), set up after the 2016 RBI Act amendment; targets **CPI inflation of 4% (±2%)**, i.e. the 2-6% band. Decisions by majority; Governor has a casting vote.

**Money supply measures:** M1 (currency + demand deposits) < M2 < M3 (broad money, most used) < M4. **M0** = reserve/high-powered money.

## 6.4 The Budget & Fiscal Policy — the government's valve

**The deficits (memorise the formulas — directly asked):**

| Deficit | Formula |
|---|---|
| **Fiscal Deficit** | Total Expenditure − Total Receipts (excluding borrowings) = the government's total borrowing need |
| **Revenue Deficit** | Revenue Expenditure − Revenue Receipts |
| **Primary Deficit** | Fiscal Deficit − Interest payments |
| **Effective Revenue Deficit** | Revenue Deficit − grants for capital asset creation |

**Budget documents under Art 112** = "Annual Financial Statement." Receipts split into **Revenue** (taxes, no asset/liability change) and **Capital** (borrowings, disinvestment). **FRBM Act, 2003** mandates fiscal discipline (target fiscal deficit ~3% of GDP). Taxes: **Direct** (income, corporate — burden can't be shifted) vs **Indirect** (GST, customs — burden shifts to consumer). **GST (101st Amendment, 1 July 2017)** — "one nation one tax," GST Council (Art 279A) chaired by Union FM; slabs 0/5/12/18/28%.

## 6.5 Inflation — when the ride spins too fast

**Types:** Demand-pull (too much money chasing goods) vs Cost-push (input costs rise). **Headline** (overall) vs **Core** (excludes food & fuel). Other terms: *Stagflation* (inflation + stagnation/unemployment together), *Deflation* (falling prices), *Disinflation* (slowing rate of inflation), *Reflation*.

**India's price indices:**

| Index | Measures | Published by | Base year |
|---|---|---|---|
| **CPI** | Retail prices (consumer) — RBI's target | NSO/MoSPI | **2024** (new series released Feb 2026; old base was 2012) |
| **WPI** | Wholesale prices | Office of Economic Adviser, DPIIT | 2011-12 |
| **CPI-IW** | Industrial workers (used for DA) | Labour Bureau | 2016 |

> **Trap:** RBI targets **CPI**, not WPI. WPI has no services component; CPI does. CPI-IW is the one used to fix **Dearness Allowance** — directly relevant to the EPF/wages world of Chapters 1-3.

## 6.6 The world window — BoP, FTAs, institutions

**Balance of Payments = Current Account + Capital Account.** Current = trade in goods (Balance of Trade) + services + transfers (remittances). Capital = FDI, FII, loans, banking capital. A **current account deficit (CAD)** means India imports more than it exports + earns. **Forex reserves** managed by RBI.

**FDI vs FII:** FDI = lasting management interest, "patient" long-term capital; FII/FPI = portfolio (stocks/bonds), "hot" money that can flee quickly.

**Global institutions:** **IMF** (short-term BoP support, SDR, HQ Washington), **World Bank** (long-term development, IBRD+IDA), **WTO** (trade rules, Geneva), **ADB** (Manila). India's FTAs/RTAs: with ASEAN, Japan (CEPA), UAE (CEPA 2022), Australia (ECTA 2022); India is **not** in RCEP or CPTPP.

## 6.7 How these are tested

- **Formula recall:** Primary deficit = fiscal deficit − interest payments; NNP = GNP − depreciation.
- **Who/what:** RBI's inflation target (4%±2%, CPI); MPC strength (6); base year of GDP (2011-12).
- **Tool direction:** "To control inflation, RBI will ___ the repo rate" → *raise*.
- **Index sorting:** CPI vs WPI vs CPI-IW; which is RBI's target; which fixes DA.
- **Concept pairs:** FDI vs FII; direct vs indirect tax; demand-pull vs cost-push.
- **Amendment/date:** GST = 101st Amendment, 1 July 2017, Art 279A Council.

## 6.8 Trap pairs

- **Repo vs Reverse Repo** — repo = RBI lends (raise to fight inflation); reverse repo = RBI borrows (raise to absorb liquidity).
- **CRR vs SLR** — CRR is cash with RBI (no interest); SLR is liquid assets held by the bank itself (earns return).
- **Fiscal vs Revenue vs Primary deficit** — know which subtracts interest (primary) and which excludes borrowings (fiscal).
- **CPI vs WPI** — RBI targets CPI; WPI has no services.
- **GDP vs GNP vs NNP** — add foreign income for GNP, subtract depreciation for NNP.
- **Nominal vs Real GDP** — real strips inflation using a base year.
- **FDI vs FII** — lasting interest vs portfolio/hot money.

## 6.9 Self-test

1. Write the formula for Primary Deficit.
2. What does the RBI target, at what level, and with how many MPC members?
3. Which index is used to calculate Dearness Allowance?
4. Differentiate CRR and SLR in one line.
5. Which Constitutional Amendment introduced GST and under which Article is the GST Council?

**Answers:** 1) **Primary Deficit = Fiscal Deficit − Interest Payments.** 2) **CPI inflation at 4% (±2%, i.e. 2-6%)**, decided by a **6-member MPC.** 3) **CPI-IW** (Consumer Price Index for Industrial Workers). 4) **CRR** = cash kept with RBI (no interest); **SLR** = liquid assets (gold/G-secs) held by the bank itself. 5) **101st Amendment (2017); GST Council under Article 279A.**

## 6.10 One sentence to carry forever

> **The economy is one money merry-go-round: the Budget is the government's hand and the repo rate is the RBI's hand on its speed; when it spins too fast you get inflation (measured by CPI), and the world window of trade is the Balance of Payments.**

# CHAPTER 7 — The Accounting Spine

## 7.0 The 60-second picture

Accounting was the single biggest topic in the APFC/EO-AO papers (ACCT_PRINCIPLES ~62 hits). It looks scary but rests on **one equation that can never break: Assets = Liabilities + Capital.** Every transaction touches that equation twice (double entry), gets recorded in a **journal**, sorted into a **ledger**, summarised in a **trial balance**, and finally shown in two statements: the **Trading & Profit-and-Loss Account** (did we earn?) and the **Balance Sheet** (what do we own & owe?). Learn the equation and the flow, and the rest is vocabulary.

## 7.1 The mental model — the accounting river

```
   TRANSACTION
       │  (recorded by Double Entry: every debit has a credit)
       ▼
   JOURNAL (the diary — chronological)
       │  posted to
       ▼
   LEDGER (accounts grouped — Cash a/c, Sales a/c...)
       │  balances listed in
       ▼
   TRIAL BALANCE (debits must equal credits — arithmetic check)
       │  adjusted, then split into
       ├──► TRADING + P&L A/C  → Net Profit / Loss  (a flow over a period)
       └──► BALANCE SHEET      → Assets = Liabilities + Capital (a snapshot)
```

Money flows down the river from a single transaction to the two final statements. The exam tests every bend: which account to debit, the golden rules, what goes in the Trading account vs P&L vs Balance Sheet.

## 7.2 The bedrock — the accounting equation & double entry

**Accounting Equation:** **Assets = Liabilities + Capital** (Owner's Equity). Rearranged: Capital = Assets − Liabilities.

**Double-entry system:** every transaction has two equal and opposite effects — one **debit (Dr)** and one **credit (Cr)**. Total debits always equal total credits; that's why a trial balance must tally.

**The Golden Rules (Traditional / British approach):**

| Account type | Rule |
|---|---|
| **Personal** (persons, firms, banks) | Debit the **receiver**, Credit the **giver** |
| **Real** (assets, cash, goods) | Debit **what comes in**, Credit **what goes out** |
| **Nominal** (expenses, incomes, gains, losses) | Debit all **expenses & losses**, Credit all **incomes & gains** |

**Modern (American) approach** uses five elements — Assets, Liabilities, Capital, Income, Expenses — with debit/credit rules per element; either approach gives the same answer.

## 7.3 The fundamental concepts & conventions (high-frequency)

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Business Entity** | Owner and business are separate; owner's capital is a liability *to* the business |
| **Going Concern** | Business will continue indefinitely; assets recorded at cost, not break-up value |
| **Money Measurement** | Only transactions measurable in money are recorded |
| **Cost Concept** | Assets recorded at purchase (historical) cost |
| **Accrual** | Revenue/expense recorded when **earned/incurred**, not when cash moves |
| **Matching** | Match expenses of a period to the revenues of the same period |
| **Dual Aspect** | Every transaction has two sides (the basis of double entry) |
| **Conservatism / Prudence** | Anticipate no profit but provide for all possible losses |
| **Consistency** | Use the same methods year to year |
| **Materiality** | Record what is significant enough to influence decisions |

> **Trap:** *Concepts* are assumptions (going concern, accrual); *Conventions* are practices (conservatism, consistency, materiality, full disclosure). Sorting the two is a stock question.

## 7.4 Books, accounts & the final statements

**Subsidiary books (special journals):** Cash Book, Purchases Book, Sales Book, Purchase Returns, Sales Returns, Bills Receivable/Payable, Journal Proper. **Cash Book** is both a journal and a ledger.

**Capital vs Revenue:** *Capital expenditure* (buying a machine — benefit over years, goes to Balance Sheet) vs *Revenue expenditure* (repairs, salaries — benefit in one year, goes to P&L). Misclassifying the two is the most common trap.

**The two final statements:**

| Statement | Answers | Contents |
|---|---|---|
| **Trading A/c** | Gross Profit? | Opening stock, purchases, direct expenses (Dr) vs Sales, closing stock (Cr) → **Gross Profit/Loss** |
| **Profit & Loss A/c** | Net Profit? | Gross Profit b/d + indirect incomes vs indirect/operating expenses → **Net Profit/Loss** |
| **Balance Sheet** | Financial position? | **Assets = Liabilities + Capital**; a *snapshot* on one date |

**Key ratios** (appear as one-liners): Gross Profit Ratio, Net Profit Ratio, Current Ratio (Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities, ideal 2:1), Quick/Acid-test Ratio (1:1), Debt-Equity Ratio.

## 7.5 Inventory, depreciation & cash flow

**Inventory valuation:** **FIFO** (First-In-First-Out — in inflation, lower COGS, higher profit), **LIFO** (Last-In-First-Out — *not allowed under Ind-AS/IFRS*), **Weighted Average**. Rule: stock valued at **cost or net realisable value, whichever is lower** (conservatism in action).

**Depreciation** (spreading an asset's cost over its life): **Straight Line Method (SLM)** — equal amount each year = (Cost − Scrap)/Life; **Written Down Value (WDV)/Diminishing Balance** — fixed % on reducing book value (higher early, lower later). Causes: wear & tear, obsolescence, efflux of time.

**Cash Flow Statement (AS-3 / Ind-AS 7)** — three activities: **Operating** (core business), **Investing** (buy/sell assets), **Financing** (raise/repay capital & loans). Don't confuse with Fund Flow (working-capital based).

## 7.6 How these are tested

- **The equation:** "If assets = 5,00,000 and liabilities = 2,00,000, capital = ?" → 3,00,000.
- **Golden rule application:** "Cash received from Ramesh — which accounts?" → Debit Cash (real, comes in), Credit Ramesh (personal, giver).
- **Concept vs convention** sorting; "which concept treats owner & business as separate?" → Business Entity.
- **Capital vs revenue** expenditure classification.
- **Statement placement:** "Closing stock appears in?" → Trading A/c (Cr) **and** Balance Sheet (asset).
- **Method facts:** LIFO banned under Ind-AS; SLM vs WDV; FIFO effect in inflation.
- **Ratio formulas:** current ratio ideal 2:1.

## 7.7 Trap pairs

- **Debit the receiver vs Debit what comes in** — receiver applies to *personal* accounts, "comes in" to *real* accounts. Apply the right rule to the right account type.
- **Concept vs Convention** — going concern/accrual are concepts; conservatism/consistency/materiality are conventions.
- **Capital vs Revenue expenditure** — machine = capital (Balance Sheet); its repair = revenue (P&L).
- **FIFO vs LIFO** — in inflation FIFO gives higher profit; LIFO is disallowed under Indian/IFRS standards.
- **SLM vs WDV depreciation** — SLM = equal each year on cost; WDV = fixed % on reducing balance.
- **Trading A/c vs P&L A/c** — gross profit (direct items) vs net profit (indirect items).
- **Reserve vs Provision** — provision is a charge against profit for a known liability/loss; reserve is an appropriation of profit.

## 7.8 Self-test

1. State the accounting equation and rearrange it for Capital.
2. Apply the golden rules: "Paid rent in cash." Which account is debited, which credited, and of what type?
3. Is "going concern" a concept or a convention? What about "conservatism"?
4. Where does closing stock appear in the final accounts?
5. Which inventory method is disallowed under Ind-AS, and which gives higher profit in times of inflation?

**Answers:** 1) **Assets = Liabilities + Capital**; so **Capital = Assets − Liabilities.** 2) **Debit Rent A/c** (nominal — expense), **Credit Cash A/c** (real — goes out). 3) Going concern = **concept**; conservatism = **convention.** 4) **Trading A/c (credit side)** and **Balance Sheet (current asset)**. 5) **LIFO** is disallowed; **FIFO** gives higher profit in inflation.

## 7.9 One sentence to carry forever

> **Assets = Liabilities + Capital can never break: every transaction flows as a double entry down the river — journal → ledger → trial balance → P&L (did we earn?) → Balance Sheet (what do we own and owe?).**

# CHAPTER 8 — Auditing Demystified

## 8.0 The 60-second picture

If accounting is *writing* the financial story (Chapter 7), auditing is *checking that the story is true*. An auditor is an independent referee who examines the books and gives one verdict — does the financial statement show a **"true and fair view"**? Everything in this chapter hangs on three pillars: **who appoints the auditor and for how long (Sec 139), what the auditor must do and report (Sec 143), and what happens when fraud is found.** Hold "independent referee giving a true-and-fair verdict" in your head and the section numbers slot in.

## 8.1 The mental model — the referee in the stadium

```
   MANAGEMENT plays the game (prepares accounts) ──► may be biased
        │
        ▼
   AUDITOR = independent referee
        │  watches against a RULEBOOK (Standards on Auditing, SAs)
        ▼
   Gives a VERDICT (Audit Report) — 4 kinds of whistle:
     ✓ Unqualified (clean)   — true & fair, no issues
     ! Qualified            — true & fair EXCEPT for...
     ✗ Adverse              — NOT true & fair
     ? Disclaimer           — cannot form an opinion (no evidence)
        │
        ▼
   If the referee spots cheating (FRAUD) above a threshold
        ──► reports to the CENTRAL GOVERNMENT (Sec 143(12))
```

The exam tests the *rulebook references* (which section, which standard) and the *four whistles* (types of audit opinion). Master those two and auditing is done.

## 8.2 The story

Auditing exists because of a simple conflict of interest: the people who *prepare* the accounts (management) are the same people whose performance those accounts measure. Left unchecked, they may dress up the numbers. After a string of corporate collapses — globally Enron (2001), in India **Satyam (2009)** — the law tightened the referee's independence: fixed tenures, mandatory rotation, and a duty to *blow the whistle to the government* on fraud rather than quietly resign. The Companies Act 2013 is the current rulebook, and the **National Financial Reporting Authority (NFRA)**, created in 2018, now polices the auditors themselves. So the modern story is: *the referee now has a referee.*

## 8.3 The facts you actually need

**Key Companies Act, 2013 sections:**

| Section | What it covers |
|---|---|
| **Sec 139** | **Appointment** of auditor — by members at AGM; first auditor within 30 days of incorporation; tenure 5 years; **rotation** mandatory for listed/prescribed companies (individual = 1 term of 5 yrs; firm = 2 terms of 5 yrs) |
| **Sec 140** | **Removal / resignation** of auditor (needs central government + special resolution) |
| **Sec 141** | **Eligibility & disqualifications** — only a Chartered Accountant; not an officer/employee of the company |
| **Sec 143** | **Powers & duties** — right to access books; duty to report; **143(12)** = report fraud to Central Government if ≥ ₹1 crore |
| **Sec 144** | Services an auditor **cannot** also provide (no bookkeeping, internal audit, etc. — to protect independence) |
| **Sec 147** | **Penalties** for contravention |
| **Sec 148** | **Cost audit** |

**The four audit opinions** (most-tested concept): **Unqualified/Clean** (true & fair) · **Qualified** ("true & fair except for…") · **Adverse** (not true & fair — material + pervasive misstatement) · **Disclaimer** (auditor cannot obtain enough evidence to form any opinion).

**Standards & bodies:** **ICAI** (Institute of Chartered Accountants of India, set up 1949) issues **Standards on Auditing (SAs)** and accounting standards; **NFRA** (2018) is the independent regulator overseeing auditors of large/listed companies; **CAG** audits government accounts (the public-sector auditor — recall Chapter 5, Art 148). **CARO 2020** (Companies Auditor's Report Order) = extra matters the auditor must report on.

**Types of audit:** Statutory (legally required), Internal (by the company's own staff, continuous), External/Independent, Cost (Sec 148), Secretarial, Performance/Efficiency, Government (CAG).

## 8.4 How these are tested

- **Section-to-function:** "Appointment of auditor is under which section?" → 139. "Reporting of fraud?" → 143(12). "Disqualifications?" → 141.
- **Rotation rule:** individual auditor 1 term, firm 2 terms (each 5 yrs) for listed companies.
- **Opinion type:** "Auditor concludes accounts are NOT true and fair → which report?" → **Adverse**. "Cannot get evidence → ?" → **Disclaimer**.
- **Who regulates auditors / who audits government:** NFRA / CAG.
- **Fraud threshold:** ₹1 crore → report to Central Government.

## 8.5 Trap pairs

- **Qualified vs Adverse vs Disclaimer** — Qualified = "except for" (mostly fine); Adverse = "not true & fair" (badly wrong); Disclaimer = "I cannot say" (no evidence). Don't confuse Adverse with Disclaimer.
- **Sec 139 vs 140 vs 141** — appointment / removal / disqualification. Sequential.
- **ICAI vs NFRA** — ICAI sets standards & trains CAs; NFRA is the independent statutory regulator (post-2018) that disciplines auditors of big companies.
- **Internal vs Statutory audit** — internal is voluntary/continuous, by own staff; statutory is mandatory, by an independent CA.
- **Accounting vs Auditing** — accounting *creates* the statements; auditing *verifies* them. The auditor does not prepare the accounts (Sec 144 bars it).

## 8.6 Self-test

1. Under which section is an auditor appointed, and what is the audit-firm rotation limit for a listed company?
2. Name the four types of audit opinion.
3. To whom and above what amount must an auditor report fraud, and under which sub-section?
4. Which body, created in 2018, regulates the auditors of large companies?
5. Who audits government accounts and under which Constitutional Article?

**Answers:** 1) **Sec 139**; an audit *firm* may serve **two terms of 5 years** (then mandatory rotation). 2) **Unqualified (clean), Qualified, Adverse, Disclaimer.** 3) **Central Government**, if fraud **≥ ₹1 crore**, under **Sec 143(12)**. 4) **NFRA** (National Financial Reporting Authority). 5) **CAG**, under **Article 148**.

## 8.7 One sentence to carry forever

> **The auditor is an independent referee who, using the Standards on Auditing as a rulebook, blows one of four whistles on whether the accounts are "true and fair" — and on fraud ≥ ₹1 crore must whistle to the Central Government itself.**

# CHAPTER 9 — Insurance & Statistics for APFC

## 9.0 The 60-second picture

Two small-but-scoring topics share this chapter because both are about **managing uncertainty with numbers.** *Insurance* pools many people's small premiums to pay the unlucky few — the EPFO world itself runs an insurance scheme (EDLI, Chapter 1). *Statistics* turns a pile of numbers into one or two summary figures — a **central value** (mean/median/mode) and a **spread** (range/variance/SD). Picture insurance as a *shared umbrella fund* and statistics as a *way to describe a crowd with two numbers*, and both topics become quick marks.

## 9.1 The mental model — the umbrella fund + the crowd snapshot

```
   INSURANCE = a shared umbrella fund
     100 people each pay ₹100  →  ₹10,000 pool
     the 2 who get caught in the rain are paid from the pool
     PRINCIPLE: "loss of the few, shared by many"
     regulated by IRDAI (insurance) / PFRDA (pensions)

   STATISTICS = describing a crowd with 2 numbers
     CENTRE: where is the crowd standing?  (Mean / Median / Mode)
     SPREAD: how scattered are they?       (Range / Variance / SD)
```

The exam asks you to *compute* a central value from a tiny data set, or to *sort* the definitions (which average is unaffected by extreme values? → median). Insurance questions are definitional (principles, types, regulator).

## 9.2 Insurance — the facts you actually need

**Core principles (frequently tested):**

| Principle | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Utmost good faith** (*uberrimae fidei*) | Both parties must disclose all material facts |
| **Insurable interest** | You can only insure something you'd suffer loss from |
| **Indemnity** | Compensation only up to actual loss (no profit from insurance) — applies to general, **not** life |
| **Subrogation** | After paying, the insurer steps into the insured's shoes to recover from a third party |
| **Contribution** | If multiple insurers cover the same risk, they share the claim |
| **Proximate cause** | The nearest/dominant cause of loss decides liability |

**Life vs General insurance:** *Life* = long-term, not a contract of indemnity (you can't value a life), pays on death/maturity. *General (non-life)* = fire, marine, motor, health — contracts of indemnity, usually annual. **LIC** (1956, nationalised life) and **GIC** (general) are the public giants.

**Regulator:** **IRDAI** (Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India), HQ **Hyderabad**, established by the IRDA Act **1999**. Pensions are regulated separately by **PFRDA** (Pension Fund Regulatory & Development Authority — runs the NPS). Recall from Chapter 1 that **EDLI** is EPFO's own life-insurance-linked scheme.

## 9.3 Statistics — the facts you actually need

**Measures of Central Tendency:**

| Measure | Definition | Formula / note |
|---|---|---|
| **Mean** | Arithmetic average | Σx ÷ n; uses every value; **affected by extremes** |
| **Median** | Middle value when arranged in order | unaffected by extreme values; positional |
| **Mode** | Most frequently occurring value | can be more than one (bimodal) |

**Empirical relation:** **Mode = 3 Median − 2 Mean** (for a moderately skewed distribution).

**Measures of Dispersion (spread):**

| Measure | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Range** | Highest − Lowest |
| **Quartile Deviation** | (Q3 − Q1) ÷ 2 |
| **Mean Deviation** | Average of absolute deviations from mean/median |
| **Variance** | Average of squared deviations = σ² |
| **Standard Deviation (SD)** | √Variance = σ; the most used measure of spread |
| **Coefficient of Variation** | (SD ÷ Mean) × 100 — compares variability of two data sets |

**Worked micro-example.** Data: 4, 6, 8, 10, 12. Mean = 40÷5 = **8**. Median (middle) = **8**. Range = 12−4 = **8**. SD: deviations −4,−2,0,2,4 → squares 16,4,0,4,16 = 40; variance = 40÷5 = 8; SD = √8 ≈ **2.83**.

## 9.4 How these are tested

- **Compute the average:** small data set → find mean/median/mode.
- **Which average resists extremes?** → Median.
- **Empirical relation:** Mode = 3 Median − 2 Mean.
- **Define a principle:** "Insurer recovering from a third party after paying = ?" → Subrogation. "No profit from insurance = ?" → Indemnity.
- **Indemnity applies to which?** General insurance, not life.
- **Regulator:** IRDAI (insurance), PFRDA (pensions); IRDAI HQ Hyderabad, est. 1999.

## 9.5 Trap pairs

- **Indemnity vs Subrogation vs Contribution** — indemnity = no profit; subrogation = insurer recovers from third party; contribution = insurers share. Easy to mix.
- **Mean vs Median vs Mode** — mean uses all values (extreme-sensitive); median is the middle (robust); mode is most frequent.
- **Variance vs SD** — SD is the *square root* of variance and is in the same unit as the data.
- **IRDAI vs PFRDA vs SEBI vs RBI** — insurance / pensions / securities-market / banking-and-currency regulators respectively.
- **Life vs General insurance** — life is not indemnity; general is.

## 9.6 Self-test

1. State the empirical relation between mode, median and mean.
2. Which measure of central tendency is least affected by extreme values?
3. Define subrogation in one line.
4. Which principle says you can profit nothing beyond your actual loss, and does it apply to life insurance?
5. Name the regulators of insurance and of pensions in India.

**Answers:** 1) **Mode = 3 Median − 2 Mean.** 2) **Median.** 3) After paying a claim, the **insurer steps into the insured's position to recover the loss from the third party responsible.** 4) **Indemnity**; it does **not** apply to life insurance. 5) Insurance = **IRDAI**; pensions = **PFRDA**.

## 9.7 One sentence to carry forever

> **Insurance is a shared umbrella fund where the loss of the few is paid by the many (regulated by IRDAI), and statistics describes any crowd with just two numbers — a centre (mean/median/mode) and a spread (range/variance/SD).**

# CHAPTER 10 — Modern India as One Narrative (1857-1947)

## 10.0 The 60-second picture

Don't memorise modern India as a list of dates — read it as **one story with three acts.** Act I: the British tighten control and Indians first revolt (1857 → Crown rule). Act II: educated Indians organise and the Congress is born, splitting into Moderates then Extremists (1885-1916). Act III: Gandhi arrives and turns elite politics into a *mass movement* (1917-1947), ending in Independence + Partition. If you can narrate the three acts in order, every date and leader finds its place.

## 10.1 The mental model — the three-act freedom film

```
   ACT I  (1757-1858)  THE GRIP TIGHTENS
     Plassey 1757 → Buxar 1764 → Company rule
     → REVOLT OF 1857 → Crown takes over (1858)

   ACT II (1858-1916) INDIANS ORGANISE
     INC founded 1885 (A.O. Hume)
     Moderates (petitions) → Partition of Bengal 1905
     → Swadeshi → Extremists (Lal-Bal-Pal)
     → Muslim League 1906 → Lucknow Pact 1916

   ACT III (1917-1947) THE MASS ERA (GANDHI)
     Champaran 1917 → Rowlatt+Jallianwala 1919
     → Non-Cooperation 1920 → Civil Disobedience/Dandi 1930
     → Quit India 1942 → INDEPENDENCE + PARTITION 1947
```

The exam picks one node from this chain ("Who started the Swadeshi movement?", "Which session split the Congress?") — knowing the *chain* lets you answer even unfamiliar nodes by position.

## 10.2 Act I — Revolt of 1857 & Crown rule

**Causes:** political (Doctrine of Lapse — Dalhousie annexing states), economic (drain of wealth), social-religious (interference, fear of conversion), military (the **greased cartridge** spark — Enfield rifle, cow/pig fat). **Began** 10 May 1857 at **Meerut**; **Mangal Pandey** at Barrackpore (29 March 1857) was the precursor. Symbolic leader: **Bahadur Shah Zafar** (last Mughal). Other leaders: Rani Lakshmibai (Jhansi), Tantia Tope, Nana Sahib, Begum Hazrat Mahal (Lucknow), Kunwar Singh (Bihar). **Result:** **Government of India Act 1858** — Company rule ended, Crown took over; Queen's Proclamation. Called the *First War of Independence* by V.D. Savarkar.

## 10.3 Act II — Congress, Moderates & Extremists

- **INC founded 1885** by **A.O. Hume**; first president **W.C. Bonnerjee** (Bombay session).
- **Moderates** (1885-1905): Dadabhai Naoroji ("Grand Old Man," *Drain Theory*), Gopal Krishna Gokhale, Pherozeshah Mehta — method: petitions, prayers ("3 P's").
- **Partition of Bengal (1905, Curzon)** → **Swadeshi & Boycott** movement → rise of **Extremists**: **Lal-Bal-Pal** (Lala Lajpat Rai, Bal Gangadhar Tilak — "Swaraj is my birthright," Bipin Chandra Pal) + Aurobindo.
- **Surat Split 1907** — Congress splits into Moderates and Extremists.
- **Muslim League founded 1906** (Dacca). **Lucknow Pact 1916** — Congress–League unity + Moderate–Extremist reunion.

## 10.4 Act III — the Gandhian mass movements

| Movement | Year | Trigger / feature |
|---|---|---|
| **Champaran Satyagraha** | 1917 | Gandhi's first in India — indigo farmers, Bihar |
| **Kheda + Ahmedabad** | 1918 | peasants & mill workers |
| **Rowlatt Act / Jallianwala Bagh** | 1919 | "no appeal, no lawyer"; Amritsar massacre (Gen. Dyer) |
| **Non-Cooperation + Khilafat** | 1920-22 | surrender titles, boycott; called off after **Chauri Chaura** (1922) |
| **Simon Commission** | 1928 | "all-white," "Go back Simon"; Lala Lajpat Rai injured |
| **Civil Disobedience / Dandi Salt March** | 1930 | 12 Mar–6 Apr, Sabarmati→Dandi, broke the salt law |
| **Round Table Conferences** | 1930-32 | London; Gandhi attended the 2nd |
| **Govt of India Act 1935** | 1935 | provincial autonomy; basis of the future Constitution |
| **Quit India (Aug Kranti)** | 1942 | "Do or Die"; mass arrests |
| **INA / Subhas Bose** | 1943-45 | "Give me blood, I'll give you freedom"; Azad Hind |
| **Independence + Partition** | 1947 | Mountbatten Plan; Indian Independence Act |

**Key sessions:** Lahore 1929 (**Purna Swaraj**, tricolour hoisted, 26 Jan 1930 as first Independence Day); Karachi 1931 (Fundamental Rights resolution).

## 10.5 How these are tested

- **Trigger-to-event:** greased cartridge → 1857; Rowlatt Act → Jallianwala; Chauri Chaura → calling off Non-Cooperation.
- **Who-said-what:** "Swaraj is my birthright" → Tilak; "Do or Die" → Gandhi (Quit India); "Give me blood…" → Bose.
- **Founders/firsts:** INC 1885 (Hume); first president Bonnerjee; Muslim League 1906.
- **Act-to-feature:** GoI Act 1858 (Crown rule), 1935 (provincial autonomy).
- **Session-to-resolution:** Lahore 1929 → Purna Swaraj.

## 10.6 Trap pairs

- **1857 leaders by place** — Jhansi (Lakshmibai), Lucknow (Begum Hazrat Mahal), Kanpur (Nana Sahib), Bihar (Kunwar Singh). Match correctly.
- **Moderates vs Extremists** — petitions (Naoroji, Gokhale) vs Swaraj agitation (Lal-Bal-Pal). Surat Split 1907.
- **Non-Cooperation (1920) vs Civil Disobedience (1930)** — first was non-violent boycott; second deliberately *broke* laws (salt).
- **GoI Act 1858 vs 1919 vs 1935** — Crown rule / dyarchy in provinces / provincial autonomy.
- **Partition of Bengal (1905) vs Partition of India (1947)** — Curzon's administrative split vs the country's division.

## 10.7 Self-test

1. What was the immediate spark of the 1857 revolt, and what Act ended Company rule?
2. Who founded the INC and who was its first president?
3. Which event led Gandhi to call off the Non-Cooperation Movement?
4. At which session and year was "Purna Swaraj" declared the goal?
5. Match: "Swaraj is my birthright" and "Do or Die" to their authors.

**Answers:** 1) The **greased cartridge** (Enfield rifle); **Government of India Act 1858** ended Company rule. 2) **A.O. Hume**; first president **W.C. Bonnerjee.** 3) The **Chauri Chaura** incident (1922). 4) **Lahore session, 1929.** 5) "Swaraj is my birthright" = **Tilak**; "Do or Die" = **Gandhi** (Quit India, 1942).

## 10.8 One sentence to carry forever

> **Modern India is a three-act film — the British grip tightens and triggers 1857, educated Indians organise into the Congress (Moderates → Extremists), and Gandhi turns it into a mass movement that ends in freedom and Partition in 1947.**

# CHAPTER 11 — Ancient & Medieval India + Culture

## 11.0 The 60-second picture

Three thousand years compress into **one timeline of empires**, each leaving a cultural fingerprint. Picture a relay of dynasties: **Indus → Vedic → Mauryas → Guptas ("Golden Age") → Delhi Sultanate → Mughals**, then the regional kingdoms. Each handed the next a legacy — script, religion, architecture, administration. You don't need every king; you need the *sequence* and each empire's **one signature achievement.** Culture (dances, music, architecture, festivals) is the examiner's favourite niche — high-yield, under-prepared.

## 11.1 The mental model — the empire relay baton

```
   INDUS VALLEY (2500 BCE) — planned cities, drainage, undeciphered script
        ▼
   VEDIC AGE (1500-600 BCE) — Vedas, varna, Sanskrit, Aryans
        ▼
   MAHAJANAPADAS → Jainism & Buddhism (6th c. BCE)
        ▼
   MAURYAS (321 BCE) — Chandragupta+Chanakya; ASHOKA, edicts, dhamma
        ▼
   GUPTAS (320 CE) — "GOLDEN AGE": zero, science, Sanskrit literature
        ▼
   HARSHA → regional kingdoms → DELHI SULTANATE (1206) — 5 dynasties
        ▼
   MUGHALS (1526 Babur) — Akbar to Aurangzeb; art, architecture
```

The exam picks one baton-pass ("Who founded the Maurya empire?", "Which Gupta ruler is the Golden Age?", "Who built the Qutub Minar?") — the relay tells you the answer's neighbourhood.

## 11.2 Ancient India — the signature facts

**Indus Valley (Harappan, c. 2500-1900 BCE):** planned grid cities, advanced **drainage**, the **Great Bath** (Mohenjodaro), granaries; sites — **Harappa & Mohenjodaro** (Pakistan), **Lothal** (dockyard, Gujarat), **Dholavira & Kalibangan** (India); script **undeciphered**; Bronze-Age, no temples/horses confirmed.

**Vedic Age:** four Vedas — **Rigveda** (oldest, hymns), Samaveda (music), Yajurveda (rituals), Atharvaveda; *varna* system; later Vedic → Upanishads.

**Religions (6th c. BCE):** **Jainism** — Mahavira (24th Tirthankara), *ahimsa*, Triratna; **Buddhism** — Gautama Buddha, **Four Noble Truths + Eightfold Path**, born Lumbini, enlightenment Bodh Gaya, first sermon Sarnath, death Kushinagar.

**Mauryas (321-185 BCE):** founded by **Chandragupta Maurya** (guided by **Chanakya/Kautilya**, author of *Arthashastra*). **Ashoka** — Kalinga War (261 BCE) → embraced Buddhism; rock & pillar **edicts** (Brahmi script); the **Lion Capital of Sarnath** = India's national emblem.

**Guptas (320-550 CE) — the "Golden Age":** **Chandragupta I, Samudragupta** ("Napoleon of India"), **Chandragupta II Vikramaditya**; concept of **zero & decimal**, **Aryabhata** (astronomy), **Kalidasa** (*Shakuntala*), Nalanda university flourished.

## 11.3 Medieval India — the signature facts

**Delhi Sultanate (1206-1526) — five dynasties:** **Slave/Mamluk** (Qutub-ud-din Aibak — began **Qutub Minar**; Iltutmish; Razia Sultana — first woman ruler) → **Khilji** (Alauddin — market reforms) → **Tughlaq** (Muhammad bin Tughlaq — capital shift to Daulatabad + token currency, both failed) → **Sayyid** → **Lodi** (Ibrahim Lodi — lost at Panipat 1526).

**Mughals (1526-1857):** **Babur** (won 1st Battle of Panipat 1526, founder) → **Humayun** → **Akbar** (greatest — *Din-i-Ilahi*, Mansabdari system, abolished *jizya*) → **Jahangir** → **Shah Jahan** (**Taj Mahal**, Red Fort) → **Aurangzeb** (largest extent, re-imposed jizya). **Vijayanagara** (Krishnadevaraya) & **Bahmani** kingdoms in the south; **Marathas** under **Shivaji** rose against the Mughals.

**Bhakti & Sufi movements:** Bhakti saints (Kabir, Tulsidas, Mirabai, Guru Nanak); Sufi orders (Chishti — Moinuddin Chishti at Ajmer) — both preached devotion over ritual, bridging communities.

## 11.4 Culture — the high-yield niche (examiner's favourite)

**Classical dances (state → dance):** Bharatanatyam (Tamil Nadu), Kathak (UP/North), Kathakali & **Mohiniyattam** (Kerala), Kuchipudi (Andhra), Odissi (Odisha), **Sattriya** (Assam), Manipuri (Manipur).

**Classical music:** **Hindustani** (North) vs **Carnatic** (South); the two great systems. Instruments and ragas occasionally asked.

**Architecture styles:** **Nagara** (North Indian temple, curvilinear shikhara), **Dravidian** (South, pyramidal gopuram), **Vesara** (hybrid, Deccan); Indo-Islamic (domes, arches) under the Sultanate/Mughals.

**Folk & festivals (under-prepared, high-return):** folk dances — Bhangra (Punjab), Garba (Gujarat), Bihu (Assam), Ghoomar (Rajasthan), Lavani (Maharashtra); festivals — Pongal, Onam, Bihu, Hornbill (Nagaland). Paintings — Madhubani (Bihar), Warli (Maharashtra), Pattachitra (Odisha), Tanjore (Tamil Nadu). UNESCO sites and GI tags are frequent picks.

## 11.5 How these are tested

- **Dynasty founder:** Maurya → Chandragupta; Mughal → Babur; Slave dynasty → Qutub-ud-din Aibak.
- **Signature achievement:** Gupta → zero/Golden Age; Ashoka → Lion Capital/edicts; Shah Jahan → Taj Mahal.
- **Dance/painting → state:** Sattriya → Assam; Madhubani → Bihar; Mohiniyattam → Kerala.
- **Buddhism milestones:** Lumbini (birth), Bodh Gaya (enlightenment), Sarnath (first sermon), Kushinagar (death).
- **Battle → year:** 1st Panipat 1526 (Babur). Kalinga 261 BCE (Ashoka).

## 11.6 Trap pairs

- **Mauryas vs Guptas** — Mauryas (Ashoka, edicts, 4th c. BCE) vs Guptas (Golden Age, science, 4th c. CE). Centuries apart, both "Chandragupta."
- **Qutub-ud-din vs Iltutmish** — Aibak *began* the Qutub Minar; Iltutmish *completed* it.
- **Nagara vs Dravidian vs Vesara** — North / South / hybrid temple styles.
- **Hindustani vs Carnatic** — North vs South classical music.
- **Bharatanatyam vs Mohiniyattam vs Kathakali** — all South but TN / Kerala-solo / Kerala-drama respectively.
- **Akbar vs Aurangzeb on jizya** — Akbar abolished it; Aurangzeb re-imposed it.

## 11.7 Self-test

1. Name two Indus Valley sites located in present-day India and the one with a dockyard.
2. Who founded the Maurya empire and who was his advisor?
3. Why are the Guptas called the "Golden Age"? Name one scientist of the era.
4. Match dances to states: Sattriya, Mohiniyattam, Kuchipudi.
5. Who began and who completed the Qutub Minar?

**Answers:** 1) **Lothal (dockyard), Dholavira, Kalibangan, Rakhigarhi** (any two in India; Lothal = dockyard). 2) **Chandragupta Maurya**, advised by **Chanakya (Kautilya).** 3) For advances in **mathematics (zero/decimal), astronomy, Sanskrit literature**; scientist = **Aryabhata.** 4) Sattriya → **Assam**, Mohiniyattam → **Kerala**, Kuchipudi → **Andhra Pradesh.** 5) Begun by **Qutub-ud-din Aibak**, completed by **Iltutmish.**

## 11.8 One sentence to carry forever

> **Indian history is an empire relay — Indus → Vedic → Mauryas → Guptas (Golden Age) → Delhi Sultanate → Mughals — and culture is its fingerprint: learn the sequence, each empire's one signature, and the state-to-dance/painting map the examiner loves.**

## Numbers you must know cold

| Topic | The number | Trap to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| EPF Act year | **1952** | Not 1955 or 1962 |
| Coverage threshold | **20+ employees** | IR Code raises to 300 but EPF Act threshold stays 20 |
| EPS wage cap | **₹15,000/month** | Not ₹6,500 (dead) or ₹21,000 (ESI threshold) |
| Employee contribution | **12%** of Basic+DA+RA | NOT 12% of gross salary |
| Employer split | **8.33% EPS + 3.67% EPF** = 12%; plus 0.50% EDLI + 0.50% Admin | Admin is on capped wage (max ₹75) |
| EDLI max payout | **₹7 lakh** | Formula-based; the 7 lakh is the cap |
| EPS pension formula | (Pensionable Salary × Service) / **70** | Denominator 70, not 12 or 60 |
| Min service for EPS pension | **10 years** | Less than 10 years → withdrawal benefit (Table D) |
| 7Q interest rate | **12% p.a.** (mandatory) | Not discretionary; always mandatory |
| 14B damages (post June 2024) | **1% per month** uniform | Old slab (5-25%) is dead |
| EPFO Ministry | **Ministry of Labour and Employment** | Not Finance or Commerce |
| EPF interest rate FY25 | **8.25%** | Declared by CBT annually |
| Gratuity formula | 15 days × years; max **₹20 lakh** | 5 years minimum service |
| Gratuity Act | **1972** | Not 1962 or 1965 |
| Bonus Act eligibility wage | **₹21,000/month** | Not ₹7,000 (that is the calculation ceiling) |
| Minimum bonus | **8.33%** of wages (or ₹100 if > 8.33% works out below) | Max 20%; payable in 8 months of accounting year |
| Maternity leave (post 2017) | **26 weeks** | Was 12 weeks before 2017 amendment |
| Trade union min members | **7** to apply | 10% or 100 (whichever less) to sustain |
| AITUC founded | **1920** — oldest union federation | BMS = 1955 = largest by membership |
| Standing Orders threshold (old IDA) | **100 workers** | IR Code 2020 = 300 workers |
| 6-week strike notice | **Public Utility Services only** (old IDA) | IR Code = 60 days for ALL industries |
| Section 7A | **Determination** (APFC's quasi-judicial power) | Not prosecution — that is Sec 14 |

## The 5-second decision tree for EPF numerical Qs

```
Q asks for EPS contribution on salary X?
  → Is X > ₹15,000?
      YES → use ₹15,000 as base → EPS = 8.33% × 15,000 = ₹1,250
      NO  → EPS = 8.33% × X

Q asks for EPF contribution (employee side)?
  → Always 12% × (Basic + DA + RA), regardless of salary

Q asks for EDLI payout?
  → Formula: 30 × average monthly wage of last 12 months + bonus (max ₹7 lakh total)
```

| Employer seeks exemption to run own trust | Section **17** |
