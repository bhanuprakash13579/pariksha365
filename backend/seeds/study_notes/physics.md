# Examiner's Blueprint — How Physics Gets Tested

<div class="examtip">

Physics questions in competitive exams fall into a predictable pattern. They are rarely about solving equations — they are almost always about conceptual recall and application. The examiner's favourite moves are:

**1. "Why does X happen?" questions** (pressure cooker, ice floating, sky colour) — these are pure understanding questions, and they appear in every paper.

**2. "Who discovered / invented X?"** — Röntgen, Faraday, Chadwick, Fleming; these appear 1-2 times per paper.

**3. Correction for vision defects** — myopia/hypermetropia and concave/convex lens. Never skipped by examiners.

**4. Motor vs Generator** (which is which, which hand rule) — a classic 1-mark gift if you know it.

**5. Sound speed and echo distance** — straightforward calculation questions.

**6. Alpha, Beta, Gamma radiation properties** — penetration, charge, what stops them.

Pure numerics (actual force calculations, wave equation solving) appear rarely. This book builds your conceptual understanding first, then gives you the numbers you must know cold.

</div>

**Priority order for your study time:**

1. Daily-life phenomena (Part X) — "why" questions; one in every paper
2. Instruments table (Part V) — 1-2 direct questions per paper
3. Discoverers and inventions (Part W) — constant presence
4. Sound: speed, echo, ranges (Part D) — easy marks
5. Light: mirror/lens types, vision defects, Rayleigh scattering (Part D)
6. Newton's laws with examples (Part A)
7. Electricity: Ohm's law, Indian supply, AC/DC (Part E)
8. Electromagnetism: motor/generator/transformer (Part E)
9. Nuclear: fission/fusion/radiation types (Part F)
10. Units, constants, formulas (Appendices)

The scientific world agreed on one system of measurement — the **International System of Units (SI)**. Here are its 7 base units, which all other units are built from.

| Quantity | Unit | Symbol |
|----------|------|--------|

| Mass | kilogram | kg |
| Time | second | s |
| Electric current | ampere | A |
| Temperature | kelvin | K |
| Amount of substance | mole | mol |
| Luminous intensity | candela | cd |

<div class="mnemonic">

Or more directly: **metre, kilogram, second, ampere, kelvin, mole, candela** — "Mr. K Sees Amphibians Killing Many Critters."

## Chapter A2 — Newton's Laws of Motion

Isaac Newton formulated these three laws in 1687 in his Principia Mathematica. They governed everything from cricket balls to rockets until Einstein refined them for extreme speeds and masses.

### First Law — Law of Inertia

An object at rest stays at rest, and an object moving at constant velocity continues moving at constant velocity, unless acted upon by an external force.

**Inertia** is the tendency of an object to resist change in its state of motion. Inertia is proportional to mass — a truck is harder to start moving than a bicycle.

<div class="examtip">

Exam applications of Newton's First Law: seat belts (body wants to continue moving when car stops), pulling a tablecloth quickly (plates stay due to inertia), coins on a card flicked away (coins fall into the glass), dust flying off a beaten carpet. Any "lurching" or "jerking" scenario = First Law.

</div>

### Second Law — F = ma

The rate of change of momentum of an object is proportional to the applied force and in the direction of the force.

Simplified: **Force = mass × acceleration**

- More force → more acceleration.
- More mass → less acceleration for same force.
- SI unit of force: newton (N). One newton accelerates 1 kg at 1 m/s².

### Third Law — Action and Reaction

For every action there is an equal and opposite reaction. The two forces act on different objects.

<div class="examtip">

Third Law applications that appear in exams: **rocket propulsion** (gas pushed backward → rocket pushed forward), **gun recoil** (bullet pushed forward → gun pushes back), **swimming** (you push water backward → water pushes you forward), **helicopter tail rotor** (counters torque from main rotor). Jet engines, walking, and even rowing a boat are Third Law applications.

</div>

### Equations of Motion (Constant Acceleration)

- v = u + at
- s = ut + ½at²
- v² = u² + 2as

Where: u = initial velocity, v = final velocity, a = acceleration, t = time, s = displacement.

## Chapter A3 — Gravitation

### Newton's Universal Law

Every two masses attract each other with a force proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them.

F = G × (m₁ × m₂) / r²

G = 6.674 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg² (Cavendish first measured this in 1798).

### Acceleration Due to Gravity (g)

- g at Earth's surface = 9.8 m/s² (use 10 for quick calculations).
- g is **less at the equator** than at the poles — because: (1) the Earth is oblate (bulges at equator, so equatorial surface is farther from the centre), and (2) rotational effect reduces effective gravity.
- g **decreases** as you go higher (away from centre).
- g **decreases** as you go deeper (less mass pulling you).
- **At Earth's centre, g = 0.**

<div class="keypoint">

Escape velocity from Earth = **11.2 km/s** (~40,300 km/h). This is the minimum speed an object needs to permanently escape Earth's gravity without further propulsion. Orbital velocity at Earth's surface ≈ 7.9 km/s — the speed needed to maintain a circular orbit just above the surface.

</div>

### Kepler's Laws of Planetary Motion

1. **Law of Orbits** — Planets move in elliptical orbits with the Sun at one focus.
2. **Law of Areas** — A line joining a planet to the Sun sweeps out equal areas in equal time intervals (planets move faster when closer to the Sun).
3. **Law of Periods** — T² ∝ r³ (square of orbital period is proportional to cube of semi-major axis).

### Satellites

- A **geostationary satellite** orbits at ~36,000 km above the equator, completing one orbit in exactly 24 hours — so it appears stationary relative to Earth's surface. Used for telecommunications and weather.
- **Weightlessness** in a satellite — the satellite and everything inside are in free fall together. The apparent weight is zero because both the person and the floor accelerate downward at the same rate.

## Chapter A4 — Work, Energy, Power

- **Work** = Force × displacement × cos(θ). Work is done only when displacement occurs in the direction of force. Unit: joule (J).
- **Kinetic Energy (KE)** = ½mv²
- **Potential Energy (gravitational PE)** = mgh
- **Power** = Work done / Time taken. Unit: watt (W). P = F × v.

### Conservation of Energy

Energy cannot be created or destroyed. It transforms from one form to another. Total energy of an isolated system is constant.

Classic example: A pendulum at its highest point has maximum PE and zero KE. At the lowest point, PE converts entirely to KE. The total (PE + KE) stays constant throughout the swing (ignoring air resistance).

# PART B — PROPERTIES OF MATTER

## Chapter B1 — Pressure, Fluids, Buoyancy

- **Pressure** = Force / Area. SI unit: pascal (Pa). A sharp knife cuts better than a blunt one because the same force is applied over a smaller area.
- **Atmospheric pressure** at sea level = 101,325 Pa ≈ 1 atm = 760 mm Hg.
- Atmospheric pressure decreases with altitude (less air column above).

### Pascal's Law

Pressure applied to an enclosed fluid transmits equally in all directions. This is why hydraulic systems (car brakes, JCB excavators, dental chairs) can multiply force enormously with a small input.

### Archimedes' Principle

The upward buoyant force on a body immersed in fluid equals the weight of fluid displaced by the body.

A steel ship floats because it is hollow — its effective density (steel + air) is less than water, so it displaces enough water for the buoyant force to equal its weight. A solid steel ball sinks because its density is much greater than water.

<div class="examtip">

Ice floats on water — this is anomalous expansion. Water expands on freezing (unusual behaviour). Ice density is 0.92 g/cm³ vs water at 1.0 g/cm³. This is why fish survive winter — ice floats and forms an insulating layer; liquid water at 4 °C stays below, where fish live.

</div>

## Chapter B2 — Surface Tension, Viscosity, Capillarity

- **Surface tension** — why water forms droplets (spherical = minimum surface area for a given volume), why insects can walk on water, why a needle can float on still water. Detergent reduces surface tension — the soap molecule disrupts the surface layer.
- **Viscosity** — internal friction in fluids. Honey > water > petrol. Viscosity decreases with temperature (why engine oil thins when hot).
- **Capillarity** — rise or fall of liquid in narrow tubes due to surface tension vs gravity. Water rises in a glass capillary (adhesion to glass > cohesion between water molecules). Mercury falls in a glass capillary. Plants use capillary action to draw water up roots and stems.

# PART C — HEAT AND THERMODYNAMICS

## Chapter C1 — Temperature Scales

| Scale | Freezing of water | Boiling of water |
|-------|-----------------|-----------------|
| Celsius (°C) | 0 | 100 |
| Fahrenheit (°F) | 32 | 212 |
| Kelvin (K) | 273.15 | 373.15 |

Conversion: °F = (9/5 × °C) + 32. Kelvin = Celsius + 273.15.

Absolute zero (0 K = −273.15 °C) — theoretically the coldest possible temperature; particles have minimum possible energy.

## Chapter C2 — Heat Transfer

| Mode | Medium needed | Mechanism | Examples |
|------|--------------|-----------|---------|
| Conduction | Solid (mainly) | Particle vibration, electron flow | Metal rod heating, iron; ice cube melting in hand |
| Convection | Fluid (liquid/gas) | Bulk movement of fluid | Boiling water, sea breeze, room air conditioning |
| Radiation | None — vacuum OK | Electromagnetic waves | Sun warming Earth, warmth from a fire |

<div class="keypoint">

The Sun's heat reaches Earth through the vacuum of space — this is radiation (electromagnetic waves). Only radiation requires no medium. This is why a thermos (vacuum flask) has a silvered interior — it prevents heat loss by all three mechanisms: vacuum stops conduction and convection, silvering reflects radiation.

</div>

## Chapter C3 — Specific Heat and Latent Heat

- **Specific heat** — heat energy needed to raise 1 kg of a substance by 1 °C (or 1 K).
  - Water: 4184 J/kg·°C — the highest among common substances. This is why coastal climates are milder — oceans absorb and release enormous heat with little temperature change.
  - Iron: ~449 J/kg·°C. Aluminium: ~900 J/kg·°C.

- **Latent heat** — heat absorbed or released during a phase change (state change) WITHOUT any temperature change.
  - Latent heat of fusion of ice: 334 J/g (ice → water at 0 °C).
  - Latent heat of vaporisation of water: 2260 J/g (water → steam at 100 °C).

<div class="examtip">

Steam burns are worse than boiling water burns at the same 100 °C — because steam releases its latent heat of vaporisation (2260 J/g) when it condenses on your skin, in addition to the heat from cooling. We sweat to cool down because sweat evaporating absorbs latent heat of vaporisation from the skin surface.

</div>

## Chapter C4 — Laws of Thermodynamics

1. **Zeroth Law** — If system A is in thermal equilibrium with system B, and B with C, then A and C are also in equilibrium. This defines temperature.
2. **First Law** — ΔU = Q − W. Energy is conserved. Heat added to a system equals change in internal energy plus work done by the system.
3. **Second Law** — Heat cannot spontaneously flow from cold to hot. Entropy (disorder) of an isolated system always increases.
4. **Third Law** — At absolute zero, entropy of a perfect crystal is zero. Absolute zero is unreachable in practice.

A refrigerator moves heat from cold to hot — but only by consuming electricity (external work). This is the Second Law in action.

# PART D — WAVES, SOUND, AND LIGHT

## Chapter D1 — Sound

Sound is a mechanical wave — it requires a medium to travel. It cannot travel through vacuum. That is why space is silent.

### Speed of Sound

| Medium | Speed (approximate) |
|--------|-------------------|
| Air at 0 °C | 331 m/s |
| Air at 20 °C | 343 m/s |
| Water | ~1500 m/s |
| Steel | ~5000 m/s |

Sound travels faster in solids than in liquids, and faster in liquids than in gases. Speed increases with temperature.

### Frequency Ranges

| Range | Frequency | Who uses it |
|-------|-----------|------------|
| Infrasonic | Below 20 Hz | Whales, elephants, earthquakes |
| Audible (human) | 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz | Humans |
| Ultrasonic | Above 20,000 Hz | Bats, dolphins, dogs; medical sonography, SONAR |

<div class="keypoint">

Echo needs a minimum distance of **17 metres** between you and the reflecting surface. Why? Sound takes 0.1 seconds to reach the reflector and return (minimum time for the human ear to distinguish echo from original). At 343 m/s, that is 343 × 0.1 / 2 = 17.15 m.

</div>

### Doppler Effect

The apparent frequency of a sound changes when the source and observer are in relative motion. When the source approaches, frequency appears higher (pitch rises). When the source moves away, frequency appears lower (pitch falls). Classic example: an ambulance siren changes pitch as it passes you.

### SONAR

Sound Navigation and Ranging — uses ultrasonic pulses to detect underwater objects and map the ocean floor. The time for the echo to return, multiplied by the speed of sound in water, gives twice the distance to the object.

---

## Chapter D2 — Light and Optics

Light is an electromagnetic wave that travels at **c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s** in vacuum. This is the fastest speed in the universe.

Speed of light in other media: water ~2.25 × 10⁸ m/s, glass ~2 × 10⁸ m/s. Light slows down in denser media.

**Refractive index (n)** = speed of light in vacuum / speed of light in medium. For glass ≈ 1.5, water ≈ 1.33, diamond ≈ 2.42.

### Reflection

- Angle of incidence = angle of reflection.
- Incident ray, reflected ray, and normal are in the same plane.

### Refraction (Snell's Law)

n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂

Light bends toward the normal when entering a denser medium and away from the normal when entering a less-dense medium.

**Total Internal Reflection (TIR)** — when light travels from a denser to a less-dense medium at an angle greater than the critical angle, it is completely reflected back. This is what gives diamonds their sparkle (very small critical angle ~24°) and what optical fibres rely on.

### Mirrors — Which Type Does What

| Mirror | Image formed | Used in |
|--------|-------------|---------|
| Plane | Same size, virtual, erect, laterally inverted | Looking glass |
| Concave (converging) | Depends on object position; real+inverted if beyond focus | Shaving mirror, headlights, dentist's mirror, reflecting telescopes |
| Convex (diverging) | Always virtual, erect, diminished | Rear-view mirrors in vehicles, security mirrors |

<div class="examtip">

Rear-view mirrors in vehicles are always convex — they give a wider field of view (the image is diminished but you see more). The "objects in mirror are closer than they appear" warning is because the convex mirror makes distant objects appear farther away. A driver checking a concave rear-view mirror would get a misleading sense of distances.

</div>

### Lenses — Which Type Does What

| Lens | Image type | Used for |
|------|-----------|---------|
| Convex (converging) | Real or virtual depending on position | Magnifying glass, cameras, projectors, microscopes, eye's own lens |
| Concave (diverging) | Always virtual, erect, diminished | Correcting myopia (short-sightedness) |

Lens formula: 1/v − 1/u = 1/f. Power of lens: P = 1/f (metres). Unit: dioptre (D).

### Defects of Vision and Corrections

| Defect | What happens | Correction |
|--------|-------------|-----------|
| Myopia (short-sightedness) | Image forms in front of retina; can see near but not far | Concave (diverging) lens |
| Hypermetropia (long-sightedness) | Image forms behind retina; can see far but not near | Convex (converging) lens |
| Presbyopia | Eye lens stiffens with age; cannot adjust | Bifocal lens |
| Astigmatism | Cornea unevenly curved | Cylindrical lens |
| Cataract | Lens becomes opaque | Surgery to replace lens |

<div class="mnemonic">

**Myopia → Concave, Hypermetropia → Convex**. Memory trick: the words **Myopia** and **Concave** both have fewer common letters to remember — just link the **M**yopic person needs a **C**oncave lens. Or: Myopia = "My can't see far" → need to diverge the light more → concave.

</div>

### Dispersion and Colour

- **Dispersion** — white light splits into its spectrum (VIBGYOR) when passing through a prism. Newton first demonstrated this in 1666.
- **VIBGYOR**: Violet, Indigo, Blue, Green, Yellow, Orange, Red — in order of decreasing wavelength (increasing frequency). Violet has highest frequency in visible range.
- **Rainbow** — caused by refraction + total internal reflection + dispersion inside raindrops.
- **Blue sky** — Rayleigh scattering. Shorter (blue) wavelengths scatter ~5× more than longer (red) wavelengths. So when sunlight enters the atmosphere, blue light scatters in all directions, making the whole sky look blue.
- **Red sunset** — at sunrise/sunset, sunlight travels through much more atmosphere to reach your eyes. Blue light has already scattered away; red, which scatters least, dominates.

<div class="keypoint">

Diamond sparkles because its refractive index is very high (2.42), giving a very small critical angle (~24°). Most light rays entering the diamond hit a face at more than 24° and undergo total internal reflection repeatedly — they bounce inside the diamond and finally exit from the top. This internal bouncing creates the famous sparkle.

</div>

### Electromagnetic Spectrum

From lowest to highest frequency (highest to lowest wavelength):

Radio → Microwaves → Infrared → Visible light → Ultraviolet → X-rays → Gamma rays

| Band | Wavelength | Key use |
|------|-----------|---------|
| Radio | km to m | AM/FM radio, TV broadcasting |
| Microwave | cm to mm | Microwave ovens, mobile phones, radar |
| Infrared (IR) | μm | TV remotes, thermal cameras, heat sensing |
| Visible | 400–700 nm | Human vision (VIBGYOR) |
| Ultraviolet (UV) | <400 nm | Sterilisation, vitamin D synthesis, fluorescence |
| X-rays | 0.01–10 nm | Medical imaging, airport security |
| Gamma | <0.01 nm | Cancer radiotherapy, sterilising medical equipment |

# PART E — ELECTRICITY AND MAGNETISM

## Chapter E1 — Current Electricity

- **Charge (q)** — unit: coulomb (C).
- **Current (I)** = charge per second = q/t. Unit: ampere (A).
- **Voltage / Potential difference (V)** — the "electrical pressure" that drives current. Unit: volt (V).
- **Resistance (R)** — opposition to current. Unit: ohm (Ω).

### Ohm's Law

**V = I × R**

At constant temperature, current through a conductor is proportional to the voltage across it.

Power consumed: **P = V × I = I²R = V²/R**. Unit: watt (W).

### Household Electricity in India

**230 V, 50 Hz, AC**. The 230 V is the RMS voltage. USA uses 120 V, 60 Hz.

- **AC (Alternating Current)** — current reverses direction periodically (50 times per second in India = 50 Hz). Used for transmission over long distances because voltage can be stepped up or down using transformers.
- **DC (Direct Current)** — current flows in one direction only. Used in batteries, electronic devices.

<div class="examtip">

Why is AC preferred for power transmission? Transformers can step up voltage for long-distance transmission (reducing current → reducing heat loss in wires: P = I²R). At the destination, step it back down for safe home use. Transformers work only on AC, not DC — because DC doesn't create a changing magnetic flux, so no induction occurs.

</div>

### Circuits and Safety

- **Fuse** — a thin wire of low-melting-point alloy that melts when current exceeds a safe limit, breaking the circuit.
- **MCB (Miniature Circuit Breaker)** — modern alternative; trips a switch instead of burning a wire; can be reset.
- **Short circuit** — accidental path of near-zero resistance; causes very high current → heat → fire.
- **Earthing** — safety connection to the ground. Any leakage current flows harmlessly into the ground instead of through a person.

---

## Chapter E2 — Magnetism and Electromagnetic Induction

### Magnets

- A bar magnet has two poles — North and South. Like poles repel; unlike poles attract.
- Earth is a giant magnet. The geographic North Pole of Earth is near the magnetic South Pole (that's why the North-seeking pole of a compass needle points north — it's attracted to Earth's magnetic south).
- Magnetic field lines run from North to South outside the magnet (and South to North inside).

### Electromagnetic Induction — Faraday's Discovery (1831)

Moving a magnet near a coil of wire induces a voltage (EMF) in the coil, and therefore a current if the circuit is complete.

<div class="keypoint">

This is the foundation of everything electrical we use. Generators in power stations, microphones, guitar pickups, wireless charging — all are applications of electromagnetic induction. Michael Faraday made this discovery in 1831, and it changed civilisation.

</div>

### Lenz's Law

The direction of the induced current is always such that it opposes the change (motion or flux change) causing it. Think of it as electromagnetic braking — nature resists change.

### Motor and Generator

| Device | Converts | Principle | Hand Rule |
|--------|---------|-----------|----------|
| Electric motor | Electrical → Mechanical | Current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field experiences a force | Fleming's Left-Hand Rule |
| Generator / Dynamo | Mechanical → Electrical | Moving conductor in a magnetic field induces current | Fleming's Right-Hand Rule |

<div class="mnemonic">

**Fleming's Left-Hand Rule for Motor**: Hold your left hand with thumb, index finger, and middle finger all at right angles to each other. Index finger = direction of magnetic Field, Middle finger = direction of Current, Thumb = direction of Thrust (motion/force on conductor). For a generator, use the Right hand with the same logic — Motion thumb, Field index, Current middle.

Motor = Left = I drive the **L**eft side. Generator = Right.

</div>

### Transformer

Changes the voltage of AC supply.

- **Step-up transformer**: increases voltage, decreases current.
- **Step-down transformer**: decreases voltage, increases current.

Works on mutual induction — the changing current in the primary coil creates a changing magnetic field that induces a current in the secondary coil. Transformers work only with AC.

# PART F — MODERN PHYSICS

## Chapter F1 — Atomic Models

| Model | Year | Proposer | Key idea |
|-------|------|---------|---------|
| Plum-pudding | 1897 | J.J. Thomson | Atom = positive sphere with electrons embedded like plums |
| Nuclear | 1911 | Ernest Rutherford | Most of atom is empty space; tiny dense nucleus at centre |
| Bohr model | 1913 | Niels Bohr | Electrons orbit in fixed quantised energy levels |
| Quantum mechanical | 1926 | Schrödinger / Heisenberg | Electrons exist as probability clouds (orbitals) |

Rutherford used the famous gold-foil (alpha-scattering) experiment — firing alpha particles at gold foil. Most passed through (empty space), some deflected slightly (electrons), and a few bounced straight back (nucleus). He concluded the atom is mostly empty with a tiny, dense, positive nucleus.

## Chapter F2 — Subatomic Particles

| Particle | Charge | Relative mass | Discoverer | Year |
|----------|--------|--------------|-----------|------|
| Electron | −1 | 1/1837 | J.J. Thomson | 1897 |
| Proton | +1 | 1 | Rutherford | 1919 |
| Neutron | 0 | ~1 (slightly > proton) | James Chadwick | 1932 |

<div class="keypoint">

The heaviest sub-atomic particle is the **neutron** (just slightly heavier than the proton). Three quick-recall facts: electron → Thomson 1897, proton → Rutherford 1919, neutron → Chadwick 1932. The first Nobel Prize in Physics (1901) went to Röntgen for X-rays.

</div>

## Chapter F3 — Radioactivity

Henri Becquerel discovered radioactivity in 1896 (from uranium). Marie and Pierre Curie then isolated polonium (named after Marie's homeland Poland) and radium in 1898. Marie Curie won two Nobel Prizes — Physics 1903 and Chemistry 1911.

### Three Types of Radiation

| Type | Symbol | Nature | Charge | Penetrating power | Stopped by |
|------|--------|--------|--------|-----------------|-----------|
| Alpha | α | Helium nucleus (2p + 2n) | +2 | Lowest | Paper, few cm of air |
| Beta | β | Fast electron | −1 | Medium | 3mm aluminium |
| Gamma | γ | High-energy EM wave (photon) | 0 | Highest | Several cm of lead |

<div class="mnemonic">

Penetrating power order: **α < β < γ** (alphabetical = weakest to strongest). What stops them: **Paper, Aluminium, Lead** — think PAL. Alpha needs a PAL to stop it (paper stops alpha). Aluminium stops beta. Lead stops gamma.

</div>

## Chapter F4 — Nuclear Reactions

### Fission

A heavy nucleus (Uranium-235 or Plutonium-239) splits into smaller fragments + 2-3 neutrons + enormous energy.

Each released neutron can trigger more fissions → chain reaction. In a nuclear reactor, control rods (cadmium or boron) absorb excess neutrons to control the rate. In an atomic bomb, the chain reaction is uncontrolled.

### Fusion

Light nuclei (hydrogen isotopes — deuterium and tritium) combine to form helium + energy.

This is the source of the Sun's energy. Fusion releases far more energy per unit mass than fission, but controlled fusion on Earth has not yet been achieved commercially. The ITER project in France aims to demonstrate controlled fusion.

<div class="examtip">

**"Sun's energy comes from nuclear fusion"** — this appears in almost every exam. Hydrogen (specifically deuterium and tritium) fuses to form helium, releasing energy according to Einstein's E = mc². The mass of the helium product is slightly less than the combined mass of the inputs — that tiny mass difference becomes an enormous amount of energy.

</div>

### E = mc²

Einstein's mass-energy equivalence. Even a tiny mass (m) holds an enormous amount of energy (c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s, so c² = 9 × 10¹⁶). Burning 1 gram of matter completely would yield 9 × 10¹³ joules — equivalent to 20,000 tons of TNT.

### Radiocarbon Dating

Carbon-14 (¹⁴C) is a radioactive isotope of carbon with half-life of **5730 years**. Living organisms maintain a constant ¹⁴C/¹²C ratio by continuously taking in carbon. When they die, ¹⁴C decays without being replenished. Measuring the remaining ¹⁴C ratio tells us the age of the organism. Used in archaeology (up to ~50,000 years back).

## Chapter F5 — Semiconductors

| Type | Resistivity | Band gap | Examples |
|------|------------|---------|---------|
| Conductor | Very low | None | Copper, silver, aluminium |
| Semiconductor | Intermediate | Small (~1 eV) | Silicon (Si), Germanium (Ge) |
| Insulator | Very high | Large | Glass, rubber, plastic |

- **N-type semiconductor** — doped with pentavalent atoms (phosphorus, arsenic); extra electrons are charge carriers.
- **P-type semiconductor** — doped with trivalent atoms (boron, aluminium); "holes" are charge carriers.
- **Diode** (P-N junction) — allows current in one direction only.
- **LED (Light Emitting Diode)** — emits light when forward current flows.
- **Transistor** — invented 1947 by Bardeen, Brattain, Shockley (Bell Labs); Nobel 1956. Functions as amplifier and switch. Foundation of all modern electronics.
- **Integrated Circuit (IC)** — many transistors on one silicon chip. Invented by Jack Kilby, 1958; Nobel 2000.

# PART X — DAILY-LIFE PHYSICS ("WHY" QUESTIONS)

Examiners love this section. One to two questions on everyday physics phenomena appear in almost every competitive exam. Learn the explanation for each.

| Phenomenon | Explanation |
|-----------|------------|
| Pressure cooker cooks faster | Higher pressure raises the boiling point of water to ~120 °C; food cooks faster at higher temperature |
| Cooking takes longer on mountains | Atmospheric pressure is lower at high altitude; water boils at lower temperature (~70 °C on Everest); food cooks slower |
| Ice floats on water | Anomalous expansion — water expands on freezing; ice density (0.92 g/cm³) < water density (1.0 g/cm³) |
| Fish survive under frozen lake | Ice forms on top and insulates the water below; liquid water at ~4 °C stays at the bottom where fish live |
| Mirage in desert | Light bends due to temperature gradient in hot air near the ground — total internal reflection of sky light |
| Stars twinkle, planets don't | Stars are point sources — atmospheric turbulence causes their single ray to flicker. Planets are extended discs — their many rays average out, no twinkling |
| Sky is blue | Rayleigh scattering — shorter (blue) wavelengths scatter much more than longer (red) wavelengths |
| Sunset and sunrise are red/orange | Sunlight travels a long path through the atmosphere; blue has already scattered away; red, which scatters least, survives to reach your eyes |
| Rainbow forms after rain | Water droplets act as tiny prisms — refraction + total internal reflection + dispersion separate white light into VIBGYOR |
| Diamond sparkles | Very high refractive index (2.42) → small critical angle (~24°) → total internal reflection of most rays → sparkle |
| Soap film shows colours | Thin-film interference — light reflects from both surfaces of the thin film; they interfere constructively/destructively at different wavelengths |
| Why we sweat to cool | Evaporation of sweat absorbs latent heat of vaporisation from the skin surface |
| Steam burn worse than water burn | Steam at 100 °C releases latent heat of vaporisation (2260 J/g) when it condenses on skin, plus heat from cooling |
| We slip on ice | Pressure of body weight on ice slightly lowers its melting point; a thin film of water forms → low friction |
| Spoon appears bent in water | Refraction — light bends when crossing from water to air; the image appears to shift |
| Lightning seen before thunder | Light travels at 3 × 10⁸ m/s, sound at ~340 m/s; light arrives almost instantly, sound takes ~3 seconds per km |
| Ceiling fan cools us | Increases evaporation of sweat from skin; does not actually cool the room air |
| Hot tea in saucer cools faster | Larger surface area increases rate of evaporation and radiation |
| Pendulum clock loses time in summer | Pendulum rod expands with heat → longer pendulum → longer period → clock runs slow |
| Birds sit on high-tension wires safely | Both feet are on the same wire at the same potential; no potential difference between them → no current through bird |
| A 3-pin plug is safer | Earth pin diverts any leakage current to the ground instead of through the user |
| Microwave heats food | Microwaves make water molecules vibrate (dielectric heating); heating from inside out |
| Helicopter needs a tail rotor | Main rotor rotation creates torque on the body (Newton's 3rd Law); tail rotor counters this torque |
| Geostationary satellite stays above same spot | Orbital period is 24 hours, same as Earth's rotation; appears stationary from Earth |
| Satellites feel weightless | Satellite and astronaut are in free fall together; apparent weight = 0 (no contact force needed) |
| Echo minimum distance is 17 m | Human ear needs 0.1 s gap to distinguish echo from original sound; at 340 m/s, minimum round trip = 34 m → 17 m one way |
| Mountaineers carry oxygen cylinders | Atmospheric pressure at high altitude is very low; partial pressure of oxygen is insufficient for breathing |
